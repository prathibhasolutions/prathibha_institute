Application of Derivatives

Derivatives as the Rate of Change

If a variable quantity \(y\) is some function of time \(t\), i.e. \(y = f(t)\), then a small change \(\Delta t\) causes a corresponding change \(\Delta y\) in \(y\).

\[\text{Average rate of change} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\]

The instantaneous rate of change is obtained as \(\Delta t \to 0\):

\[\lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} = \frac{dy}{dt}\]

Similarly, \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is the rate of change of \(y\) relative to \(x\). It is positive if \(y\) increases as \(x\) increases, and negative if \(y\) decreases as \(x\) increases.

Rate of Change of Two Variables

If two variables \(x\) and \(y\) both vary with another variable \(t\), i.e. \(y = f(t)\) and \(x = g(t)\), then:

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} = \frac{dy}{dt} \times \frac{dt}{dx}\]
Marginal Cost & Marginal Revenue
  • Marginal Cost (MC): Instantaneous rate of change of total cost w.r.t. number of items produced. \[\text{MC} = \frac{d}{dx}\{C(x)\}\]
  • Marginal Revenue (MR): Rate of change of total revenue w.r.t. number of items sold. \[\text{MR} = \frac{d}{dx}\{R(x)\}\]
  • Total cost: \(C(x) = \text{fixed cost} + \text{variable cost}\)

Tangents and Normals

A tangent is a straight line that touches the curve \(y = f(x)\) at a point. A normal is a straight line perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact.

Slope of Tangent and Normal
  • Slope of tangent at \(P(x,y)\) \(= \left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P\)
  • Tangent parallel to Y-axis (perpendicular to X-axis): \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = \infty\)
  • Tangent parallel to X-axis (perpendicular to Y-axis): \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = 0\)
  • Slope of normal \(= -\dfrac{1}{\text{slope of tangent}} = -\left.\dfrac{dx}{dy}\right|_P\)
  • If \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = 0\): normal is parallel to Y-axis.
  • If \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = \infty\): normal is parallel to X-axis.
Equations of Tangent and Normal

At point \(P(x_0, y_0)\) on curve \(y = f(x)\):

  • Equation of tangent: \[Y - y_0 = \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_P (X - x_0)\]
  • Equation of normal: \[Y - y_0 = -\left.\frac{dx}{dy}\right|_P (X - x_0)\]
  • If \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = 0\): tangent is \(Y = y_0\) (horizontal); normal is \(X = x_0\) (vertical).
  • If \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_P = \pm\infty\): tangent is \(X = x_0\) (vertical); normal is \(Y = y_0\) (horizontal).
Lengths of Tangent, Normal, Subtangent & Subnormal

At a point \(P(x, y)\) on the curve, where the tangent makes angle \(\theta\) with X-axis:

QuantityFormula
Length of tangent (PA) \(\displaystyle y\,\text{cosec}\,\theta = y\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\bigg/\frac{dy}{dx}\)
Length of normal (PB) \(\displaystyle y\,\sec\theta = y\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\)
Length of subtangent (AS) \(\displaystyle y\cot\theta = \frac{y}{dy/dx}\)
Length of subnormal (BS) \(\displaystyle y\tan\theta = y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}\)

Angle of Intersection of Two Curves

Let \(y = f_1(x)\) and \(y = f_2(x)\) meet at point \(P(x_1, y_1)\). The angle of intersection is the angle between their tangents at \(P\):

\[\tan\theta = \left|\frac{m_1 - m_2}{1 + m_1 m_2}\right|\]

where \(m_1 = \left.\dfrac{df_1}{dx}\right|_{(x_1,y_1)}\) and \(m_2 = \left.\dfrac{df_2}{dx}\right|_{(x_1,y_1)}\).

Special Cases
  • Orthogonal curves (\(\theta = 90°\)): \(m_1 m_2 = -1\), i.e. \(\left.\dfrac{df_1}{dx}\right|_P \cdot \left.\dfrac{df_2}{dx}\right|_P = -1\)
  • Tangential curves (\(\theta = 0°\)): \(m_1 = m_2\), i.e. curves touch each other at \(P\).

Rolle's Theorem & Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem

Rolle's Theorem

Let \(f\) be defined on \([a,b]\) such that:

  1. \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\).
  2. \(f\) is differentiable on \((a,b)\).
  3. \(f(a) = f(b)\).

Then, there exists at least one point \(c \in (a,b)\) such that \(f'(c) = 0\).

Geometric meaning: The tangent to the graph at some interior point is parallel to the X-axis.

Algebraic interpretation: Between any two roots of a polynomial \(f(x)\), there is always a root of \(f'(x)\).

Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem (LMVT)

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then, there exists at least one point \(c \in (a,b)\) such that:

\[f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\]

Geometric meaning: For any chord of the curve \(y = f(x)\), there is a point on the graph where the tangent is parallel to that chord.

Note: Rolle's theorem is a special case of LMVT when \(f(a) = f(b)\).

Approximations and Errors

Let \(y = f(x)\) and \(\Delta x\) be a small increment in \(x\). Then for small increments:

\[\Delta y \approx dy = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\Delta x\] \[f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\Delta x\]
Types of Errors

If \(\Delta x\) is the error in measuring \(x\), then the error \(\Delta y\) in \(y\) is:

\[\Delta y = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\Delta x\]
Type of ErrorFormula
Absolute error in \(y\)\(\Delta y\)
Relative error in \(y\)\(\dfrac{\Delta y}{y}\)
Percentage error in \(y\)\(\dfrac{\Delta y}{y} \times 100\%\)

Increasing and Decreasing Functions

Definitions
  • Increasing (non-decreasing): \(f\) is increasing on domain \(D\) if \(x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) \le f(x_2)\) for all \(x_1, x_2 \in D\).
  • Strictly increasing: \(x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) < f(x_2)\).
  • Decreasing (non-increasing): \(x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) \ge f(x_2)\).
  • Strictly decreasing: \(x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) > f(x_2)\).
  • Monotonic: A function that is either entirely increasing or entirely decreasing on an interval.
Derivative Conditions for Monotonicity

Let \(f\) be differentiable on \((a,b)\):

  • \(f'(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in (a,b)\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f\) is strictly increasing on \((a,b)\).
  • \(f'(x) < 0\) for all \(x \in (a,b)\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \((a,b)\).
  • \(f'(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x \in (a,b)\) (and \(f'(x) = 0\) only at finitely many points) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f\) is increasing on \((a,b)\).

Injectivity test: A strictly monotonic function is always one-one (injective). So \(f\) is one-one on \([a,b]\) if \(f'(x) > 0\) or \(f'(x) < 0\) for all \(x \in [a,b]\).

Properties of Monotonic Functions
  • If \(f\) is strictly increasing (decreasing) on \([a,b]\), then \(f^{-1}\) exists and is also strictly increasing (decreasing).
  • If \(f\) and \(g\) are both strictly increasing (or both strictly decreasing) on \([a,b]\), then \(g \circ f\) and \(f \circ g\) are strictly increasing.
  • If one of \(f\) or \(g\) is strictly increasing and the other strictly decreasing, then \(g \circ f\) and \(f \circ g\) are strictly decreasing.

Maxima and Minima of Functions

Definitions
  • Local maximum: \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = a\) if \(f(x) \le f(a)\) for all \(x \in (a-h, a+h)\), \(h\) small and positive.
  • Local minimum: \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = a\) if \(f(x) \ge f(a)\) for all \(x \in (a-h, a+h)\).
  • Critical points: Points where \(f'(x) = 0\) or \(f'(x)\) does not exist. Maxima/minima can only occur at critical points.
  • Extreme value: \(f(c)\) is an extreme value if it is either a local maximum or local minimum. At an extreme point \(a\), \(f'(a) = 0\) (if it exists).
Properties of Maxima and Minima
  • In a continuous function, at least one maxima and one minima must lie between two equal values of \(f(x)\).
  • Maxima and minima occur alternately: between two maxima there is one minima and vice versa.
  • If \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to a\) or \(x \to b\), and \(f'(x) = 0\) for only one value \(c\), then \(f(c)\) is the minimum value.
  • If \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to a\) or \(x \to b\), and \(f'(c) = 0\) for only one value \(c\), then \(f(c)\) is the maximum value.
1. First Derivative Test

Let \(f\) be differentiable on \(I\) and \(a \in I\) with \(f'(a) = 0\):

Sign of \(f'(x)\)Left of \(a\)Right of \(a\)Conclusion
Changes \(+ \to -\)\(f'(x) > 0\)\(f'(x) < 0\)Local maximum at \(x = a\)
Changes \(- \to +\)\(f'(x) < 0\)\(f'(x) > 0\)Local minimum at \(x = a\)

2. Second Derivative Test

Let \(f'(a) = 0\) and \(f''(a)\) exist:

ConditionConclusion
\(f'(a) = 0\) and \(f''(a) < 0\)Local maximum at \(x = a\)

3. \(n\)th Derivative Test

Let \(f'(a) = f''(a) = \cdots = f^{(n-1)}(a) = 0\) and \(f^{(n)}(a) \ne 0\):

  • If \(n\) is even and \(f^{(n)}(a) < 0\) \(\Rightarrow\) local maximum at \(x = a\).
  • If \(n\) is even and \(f^{(n)}(a) > 0\) \(\Rightarrow\) local minimum at \(x = a\).
  • If \(n\) is odd \(\Rightarrow\) neither local maximum nor local minimum (point of inflection).

Global Maximum and Minimum

The global (absolute) maximum/minimum of \(f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is the greatest/least value of \(f\) on that interval.

It always occurs either at a critical point of \(f\) within \([a,b]\) or at an endpoint of the interval.

Steps to Find Global Extrema on \([a,b]\)
  1. Find all critical points \(c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n\) in \([a,b]\) (where \(f'(x) = 0\) or \(f'(x)\) does not exist).
  2. Evaluate \(f\) at all critical points and at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\).
  3. The greatest value is the global maximum: \[M_1 = \max\{f(a),\, f(c_1),\, f(c_2),\, \ldots,\, f(c_n),\, f(b)\}\]
  4. The least value is the global minimum: \[M_2 = \min\{f(a),\, f(c_1),\, f(c_2),\, \ldots,\, f(c_n),\, f(b)\}\]

Important Points to Remember

  • If \(f(x)\) is differentiable, then \(f'(x)\) vanishes at every local maximum and every local minimum.
  • The converse is not true: \(f'(c) = 0\) does not necessarily mean \(c\) is a local maximum or minimum (e.g. \(f(x) = x^3\) at \(x = 0\)). Such points are called points of inflection.
  • A function may have an extreme value at a point where it is not differentiable (e.g. \(f(x) = |x|\) at \(x = 0\)).
  • A function can have several local maxima and minima in an interval; a local maximum at one point may be less than a local minimum at another point.
  • \(f'(a) = 0\) is a necessary condition for an extreme value, but not sufficient.
  • Geometrically, at a maximum or minimum, the tangent to the curve is parallel to the X-axis.
  • Points where \(f'(x) = 0\) are called stationary values (critical values) of \(x\); the corresponding \(f\)-values are called stationary (turning) values of \(f\).
  • Range of a continuous function on \([a,b]\): If the least value is \(m\) and greatest is \(M\), then range \(= [m, M]\).
  • Extreme points (turning points): Points where local maxima or minima occur. The corresponding values are called extreme values of \(f\).
