Chemical Equilibrium

Introduction, Processes & Types of Reactions

In a closed vessel at a fixed temperature, reactant concentration decreases and product concentration increases initially. A stage is eventually reached where no net change occurs — this is the state of equilibrium.

Physical vs Chemical Processes
  • Physical processes: Change only physical properties, not chemical composition (e.g. melting, evaporation).
  • Chemical processes: Involve changes in chemical composition and properties.
Types of Chemical Reactions
  • Decomposition: A compound breaks into two or more substances. e.g. PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g). Thermal decomposition (heat) or photochemical decomposition (light).
  • Displacement: One element/group replaces another (redox reactions). e.g. Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g).
  • Double Displacement (Metathesis): Two compounds exchange ions; no change in oxidation state. e.g. AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).

Physical vs Chemical Equilibrium
  • Physical Equilibrium: Set up in physical processes (evaporation, melting, dissolution). e.g. Ice ⇌ Water. At equilibrium: Rate of melting = Rate of freezing.
  • Chemical Equilibrium: In a closed vessel, rate of forward reaction equals rate of backward reaction. Rf = Rb.

Characteristics of Equilibrium & Types

Characteristics of Chemical Equilibrium
  1. Equilibrium can be attained from either side (forward or backward).
  2. It is dynamic in nature — the reaction does not stop at equilibrium.
  3. Concentrations of all species remain constant at equilibrium.
  4. A catalyst does not change the equilibrium position — it only helps to attain it faster.
  5. Equilibrium can be achieved only in a closed container.
  6. Observable physical properties (density, pressure, colour) become constant.
Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous Equilibrium
TypeDefinitionExample
Homogeneous Reactants and products in the same phase 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)
Heterogeneous Reactants and products in different phases 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) ⇌ Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

Note: In heterogeneous equilibria, the active mass of pure solids and pure liquids is taken as 1 (constant).

Law of Mass Action & Equilibrium Constants

Guldberg and Waage's Law of Mass Action: The rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of the active masses of the reacting substances.

For the general reaction: \(aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD\)

\[K_c = \frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}\]
Kp and Relation with Kc

For gaseous reactions, using partial pressures:

\[K_p = \frac{p_C^c \cdot p_D^d}{p_A^a \cdot p_B^b}\] \[K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\]

where \(\Delta n_g\) = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants.

ConditionRelationship
\(\Delta n_g = 0\)\(K_p = K_c\)
\(\Delta n_g > 0\)\(K_p > K_c\)
\(\Delta n_g < 0\)\(K_p < K_c\)
  • Unit of \(K_p = (\text{atm})^{\Delta n}\)
  • Unit of \(K_c = (\text{mol L}^{-1})^{\Delta n}\)
Characteristics of Equilibrium Constant
  1. Has a definite value for every reaction at a particular temperature.
  2. Larger \(K_c\) → greater extent of completion. (\(K \ge 10^3\): completion; \(K \le 10^{-3}\): reaction barely proceeds).
  3. When a reaction = sum of two reactions, its \(K_c\) = product of individual \(K_c\) values.
  4. Independent of initial concentrations and presence of catalyst.
  5. \(K_c\) for reverse reaction = \(\dfrac{1}{K_c}\) for forward reaction.
  6. If equation is multiplied by \(n\): new \(K = K^n\); divided by \(m\): new \(K = K^{1/m}\).
Reaction Quotient (Qc)

At any stage other than equilibrium: \(Q_c = \dfrac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}\)

ConditionDirection of Reaction
\(Q_c > K_c\)Reaction proceeds backward (products → reactants)
\(Q_c < K_c\)Reaction proceeds forward (reactants → products)
\(Q_c = K_c\)System is at equilibrium
Relation with Gibbs Free Energy
\[\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + 2.303RT\log Q_c\]

At equilibrium: \(\Delta G = 0\) and \(Q_c = K_c\), therefore:

\[\Delta G^\circ = -2.303RT\log K_c = -2.303RT\log K_p \text{ (ideal gases)}\]

Le-Chatelier's Principle

Le-Chatelier's Principle: If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in concentration, pressure, or temperature, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that tends to nullify the effect of the change.

Effect of Various Factors
Factor ChangedEffect on Equilibrium
Increase in concentration of reactant Shifts forward (to consume added reactant)
Increase in pressure Shifts toward side with fewer moles of gas. No effect if \(\Delta n_g = 0\).
Increase in temperature (exothermic rxn) Shifts backward (low T favours forward)
Increase in temperature (endothermic rxn) Shifts forward (high T favours forward)
Addition of inert gas at constant pressure Volume increases → shifts toward side with more moles of gas
Addition of inert gas at constant volume No effect on equilibrium position
Catalyst Does not change position — only speeds up attainment of equilibrium
Le-Chatelier's Principle in Physical Equilibria
  • Solubility of gases: Increased pressure → increased solubility (Henry's Law).
  • Solubility of solids (endothermic dissolution): Increased temperature → increased solubility (e.g. NH₄Cl, KCl, KNO₃). Exothermic dissolution (e.g. Ca(OH)₂) shows opposite trend.
  • Melting point of ice: Ice ⇌ Water. Ice occupies more volume, so increased pressure favours melting → ice melts at lower temperature under high pressure.
