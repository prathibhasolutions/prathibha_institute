Part A — d-Block Elements (Transition Metals)
⚛️ Introduction & Electronic Configurations

d-block contains elements of groups 3–12 in which d-orbitals are progressively filled. Three main series: 3d (Sc–Zn), 4d (Y–Cd), 5d (La–Hg, omitting Ce–Lu). A fourth 6d series begins at Ac.

Transition elements: Elements with partially filled d-orbitals in ground state OR in excited state.

Zn, Cd, Hg have d¹⁰s² configuration — do NOT show transition element properties → called non-typical transition elements. Iron is the most abundant and widely used transition metal.

📊 General Properties of Transition Elements
(i) Atomic & Ionic Size
  • Ions of same charge in a series show regular decrease in radius as atomic number increases (new electron enters d-orbital, nuclear charge increases).
  • At end of series, slight increase due to electron-electron repulsion.
  • Radii increase from 3d → 4d series; 3d and 5d have similar radii due to lanthanoid contraction (poor shielding of 4f).
(ii) Ionisation Enthalpies
  • Increases across a period (increasing nuclear charge) but not in regular trend.
  • Irregular trend due to relative energies of 4s and 3d orbitals.
  • The low value for Sc³⁺/Sc²⁺ → Sc³⁺ has noble gas configuration.
  • Highest value for Zn³⁺/Zn²⁺ → removal of electron from stable d¹⁰.
(iii) Oxidation States
  • Show variable oxidation states due to two incomplete outermost shells (3d and 4s).
  • Lower oxidation states (+2, +3) → ionic compounds.
  • Higher oxidation states → normally covalent.
  • Only Os and Ru show +8 oxidation state (in fluorides and oxides).
  • Ni in Ni(CO)₄ and Fe in Fe(CO)₅ show zero oxidation state.
Stable oxidation states: Sc(+3), Ti(+4), V(+5), Cr(+3,+6) Mn(+2,+7), Fe(+2,+3), Co(+2,+3) Ni(+2), Cu(+2), Zn(+2)
(iv–v) Enthalpy & Standard Electrode Potential
  • High enthalpies of atomisation due to large number of unpaired electrons → stronger interatomic interactions.
  • E°(M²⁺/M) governed by: enthalpy of sublimation, ionisation enthalpy, hydration enthalpy.
  • Except copper, all 3d series elements are good reducing agents (E° negative).
(vi) Melting & Boiling Points
  • High m.p. and b.p. due to strong metallic bonding.
  • Maximum m.p. in the middle of series; decreases with increasing atomic number.
  • Mn and Tc show abnormal values.
  • Tungsten has highest m.p. (3410°C) of all metals.
  • Mercury is liquid at room temperature (m.p. = −38.9°C) due to absence of unpaired electrons → weak metallic bonding.
(vii) Density

High density due to small atomic size and strong metallic bonding.

(xi) Magnetic Properties
  • Paramagnetic: due to unpaired electrons in d-orbitals. Increases with number of unpaired electrons (n). Highest paramagnetic character: Mn²⁺ (5 unpaired e⁻) among 3d series.
  • Diamagnetic: repelled by magnetic field; no unpaired electrons.
  • Ferromagnetic: permanent magnetic character. e.g. Fe, Co, Ni.
Magnetic moment: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM (Bohr magneton) n = number of unpaired electrons
(xii) Coloured Ions
  • Colour due to d-d transitions (unpaired electrons in d-orbitals absorb visible light → electron excited from lower to higher d-orbital).
  • Colour depends on metal, oxidation state, and ligands.
  • [Cu(H₂O)₄]²⁺: pale blue; [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺: dark blue; CuSO₄·5H₂O: blue; anhydrous CuSO₄: colourless.
  • Charge transfer: MnO₄⁻ (d⁰) has purple colour due to O→Mn charge transfer.
(xiii) Catalytic Properties
Due to (a) variable oxidation states; (b) formation of intermediate complexes; (c) rough surface area → active sites.

(xv–xvi) Interstitial & Non-stoichiometric Compounds
  • Interstitial compounds: Small non-metal atoms (H, N, C) occupy voids in crystal lattice → hard and rigid. e.g. cast iron, steel.
  • Non-stoichiometric: Elements not in exact ratio. e.g. Fe₀.₉₄O (Fe:O ≈ 0.94:1) due to variable oxidation state of transition metals.
  • Spinel: Mixed oxides where O atoms form fcc lattice. e.g. ZnFe₂O₄ (normal spinel: Fe³⁺ in octahedral holes, Zn²⁺ in tetrahedral holes); Fe₃O₄ (inverse spinel).
💫 Alloys of d-Block Elements

d-block elements form alloys easily due to similar atomic sizes. Alloys have high m.p. Mo, W, Cr, Ni, V used for stainless steel. Amalgam = alloy with Hg (Fe and Pt do NOT form amalgam).

Copper Sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) — Blue Vitriol
2Cu + 2H₂SO₄ + O₂ → 2CuSO₄ + 2H₂O (air + dil H₂SO₄ on copper scrap)
  • Heating → turns white (loss of water of crystallisation).
  • At 1000K: CuSO₄ → CuO + SO₃
  • Gives blue solution with NH₄OH; white ppt of Cu₂I₂ with KI.
  • Uses: Electroplating, mordant in dyeing, Bordeaux mixture [Ca(OH)₂ + CuSO₄].
Silver Nitrate (AgNO₃) — Lunar Caustic
3Ag(s) + 4HNO₃(dil) → 3AgNO₃ + NO + 2H₂O
  • Colourless crystalline; blackens on contact with organic matter (skin, cloth).
  • With K₂Cr₂O₇ → red ppt of Ag₂CrO₄.
  • On strong heating: 2AgNO₃ → 2Ag + 2NO₂ + O₂
  • Ammoniacal AgNO₃ = Tollen's reagent [Ag(NH₃)₂]OH.
  • Uses: Laboratory reagent, silvering mirrors, inks, hair dyes.

Part B — f-Block Elements (Inner-Transition Metals)
🌏 f-Block Elements — Introduction

Elements in which filling of electrons takes place in f-subshells (two levels inside outer subshell) are called inner-transition elements.

4f-Series: Lanthanoids (Lanthanides)
  • Elements 57 (La) to 71 (Lu); also called rare earths.
  • Last electron enters 4f orbital.
  • La [Xe]4f⁰5d¹6s² and Lu [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹6s² have no partially filled 4f orbital in ground state but are still classified as lanthanoids.
General e⁻ config: [Xe] 4f⁰⁻¹⁴ 5d⁰⁻¹ 6s²
5f-Series: Actinoids (Actinides)
  • Elements 89 (Ac) to 103 (Lr); constitute the 5f-series.
  • From neptunium (Np, 93) onwards: man-made (artificially prepared), also called transuranic elements.
  • Little difference between 5f and 6d energies → configuration harder to predict.
General e⁻ config: [Rn] 5f⁰⁻¹⁴ 6d⁰⁻¹ 7s²
🧲 Lanthanoids — Detailed Properties
Oxidation States

Most common and most stable: +3. Some show +2 and +4 (by leaving behind stable ions).

Magnetic Properties

Magnetic moments have BOTH spin and orbital contributions:

μ = √[4S(S+1) + L(L+1)] (L = orbital quantum number, S = spin quantum number) For first-row transition metals, orbital contribution is quenched → use spin-only formula: μₛ = √[n(n+2)] BM

All lanthanoid ions except La³⁺, Lu³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺ are paramagnetic.

Lanthanoid Contraction ★★★

Steady decrease in atomic and ionic (Ln³⁺) radii as atomic number of lanthanoids increases.

Cause: Additional electron goes to 4f-subshell. 4f orbitals are large and diffuse → poor shielding effect. Effective nuclear charge increases → contraction of electron cloud. Contraction is quite regular.
Consequences of Lanthanoid Contraction:
  1. Covalent character of cations increases.
  2. Electronegativity of trivalent ions increases slightly.
  3. Basic strength of oxides and hydroxides decreases from La to Lu.
  4. Small increase in standard electrode potential values.
  5. Sizes of Zr and Hf are similar; Nb and Ta are similar → called chemical twins (due to lanthanoid contraction of lanthanoids in 4f series before 5d series).
Other Properties
  • Colour: Species with unpaired electrons are coloured; f-f transitions from visible region absorption.
  • M.p. and B.p.: High values but no regular trend.
  • Density: 6.67–9.7 g/cm³; no regular trend.
  • Electronegativity: Same as s-block elements → form ionic compounds.
  • Ionisation energy: Low (large size) → comparable to alkaline earth metals → very reactive.
  • Complex formation: Little tendency due to large ionic size.

⚜️ Actinoids — Properties & Comparison
Key Properties
  • Oxidation states: +3 (most common) up to +7 (maximum).
  • Actinoid contraction: Similar to lanthanoid contraction; due to poor shielding of 5f electrons. Greater than lanthanoid contraction.
  • Magnetic properties: Magnetic moments smaller than theoretical; hard to interpret due to large spin-orbit coupling.
  • Colour: Actinoid ions are coloured due to unpaired electrons and f-f transitions.
  • Complex formation: Higher tendency to form complexes than lanthanoids.
  • Reducing character: Strong reducing agents (E° ≈ −2.0 V).
  • Reactivity: Very reactive; combine with O₂ and halogens like lanthanoids.
