Chapter 24 — General Organic Chemistry
🧪 Introduction to Organic Chemistry

The hydrides of carbon (hydrocarbons) and their derivatives are called organic compounds. The branch of chemistry dealing with these compounds is called organic chemistry.

Historical Background
  • Berzelius (1808) defined organic chemistry as the chemistry of substances found in living matter — gave the Vital Force Theory.
  • Wohler (1828) synthesised urea — the first organic compound made in laboratory — delivered death blow to vital force theory:
    (NH4)2SO4 + 2KCNO → 2NH4CNO → NH2CONH2 (urea) - K2SO4
  • Acetic acid is the first organic compound synthesised from its elements.
Reasons for Large Number of Organic Compounds
  • (a) Catenation: Tendency of self-combination is maximum in carbon. Carbon atoms can combine by single, double or triple bonds, forming more compounds than any other element.
  • (b) Tetravalency & Small Size: Carbon is tetravalent — can bond with four other C-atoms or monovalent atoms. Forms compounds with O, H, Cl, S, N, P. Exceptionally stable due to small size of carbon.
General Characteristics of Organic Compounds
  1. These are compounds of carbon with H, O, N, S, P, F, Cl, Br and I.
  2. Generally found in living organisms (e.g. carbohydrates, proteins).
  3. May be gases, liquids or solids.
  4. Covalent in nature → low boiling point and melting point; soluble in organic solvents.
  5. Generally volatile and inflammable.
  6. Do not conduct electricity (absence of free ions).
  7. Possess distinct colour and odour.
Representation of Organic Formulas
1. Complete Formula
All bonds between every two atoms shown clearly.
H H H H | | | | H-C-C-C-C-H | | | | H Cl H H
2. Condensed Formula
Bonds not shown in full.
CH3CHClCH2CH3 or CH3CH(Cl)CH2CH3
3. Bond-Line Formula
Every fold and free terminal = carbon; lines = bonds. H-atoms assumed present to satisfy tetravalency.
Zig-zag lines; each vertex/terminal = C (H atoms not explicitly shown)
3D Representation: Solid wedge (filled triangle) = bond towards observer; Dashed wedge (dashed lines) = bond away from observer. Used to show 3-D structure of molecules like methane on paper (wedge-and-dash representation).
🌿 Classification of Organic Compounds
Open Chain (Acyclic / Aliphatic)
Compounds with open carbon chains: alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, etc.
Closed Chain (Cyclic / Ring)
  • Homocyclic (Carbocyclic): Ring contains only carbon atoms.
    • Alicyclic: Cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclohexane (non-aromatic)
    • Aromatic Benzenoid: Benzene, naphthalene
    • Non-benzenoid: Tropolone
  • Heterocyclic: Ring contains O, N, or S (e.g. furan, pyrrole, thiophene — all aromatic)
Classification of Carbon Atoms
Based on number of carbons attached:
  • 1° (Primary): Attached to one other C-atom
  • 2° (Secondary): Attached to two other C-atoms
  • 3° (Tertiary): Attached to three other C-atoms
  • 4° (Quaternary): Attached to four other C-atoms
Reactivity order: 3° > 2° > 1°
Based on position of functional group:
  • alpha-carbon: Directly attached to the functional group
  • beta-carbon: Attached to alpha-carbon
Classification of Hydrogen Atoms
  • 1° H (primary): Attached to 1°-carbon
  • 2° H (secondary): Attached to 2°-carbon
  • 3° H (tertiary): Attached to 3°-carbon
  • alpha-hydrogen: H attached to alpha-carbon
  • beta-hydrogen: H attached to beta-carbon
Examples: beta alpha CH3 - CH2 - Cl beta alpha CH3 - CH2 - COOH
⚗️ Functional Group & Homologous Series
Functional Group

The atom (e.g. -Cl, -Br) or group of atoms (e.g. -COOH, -CHO) responsible for the chemical properties of the molecule is called the functional group.

  • Double and triple bonds are also functional groups.
  • R- = alkyl group (only single bonds); alkenyl group (double bond); alkynyl group (triple bond).
Homologous Series

A series in which adjacent members differ by a -CH2 unit (14 mass units). Members are called homologues.

SeriesGeneral Formula

All homologues: same functional group → similar chemical properties; similar preparation methods; physical properties vary gradually.

🔄 Isomerism

Compounds having the same molecular formula but differ in properties are called isomers; the phenomenon is isomerism.

1. Structural Isomerism (same molecular formula, different structure)
(i) Chain Isomerism: Different carbon skeletons.
C5H12: n-pentane, iso-pentane, neo-pentane
(ii) Position Isomerism: Different position of functional group/substituent.
CH3CH2CH2Cl vs CH3CHClCH3
(iii) Functional Isomerism: Different functional groups.
CH3CH2CHO (propanal) vs CH3COCH3 (propanone) C2H5OH vs CH3-O-CH3
(iv) Metamerism: Different alkyl groups on either side of same functional group.
CH3-O-C3H7 vs C2H5-O-C2H5
(v) Tautomerism: Special type in carbonyl compounds containing alpha-H.
CH3-C(=O)-H ⇌ CH2=C(OH)-H (keto form) (enol form)
2. Stereoisomerism (same molecular formula, different spatial arrangement)
(i) Optical Isomerism

Compounds differing only in behaviour towards plane-polarised light are called enantiomers or optical isomers.

  • Dextrorotatory (d / +): Rotates plane of polarised light clockwise.
  • Laevorotatory (l / -): Rotates plane anti-clockwise.
  • Chiral centre: Carbon with all four valencies satisfied by four different groups.
  • Allenes, spiranes, and biphenyl compounds are also optically active despite having no chiral centre (asymmetric overall).
Number of optical isomers = 2^n (n = chiral carbons) If two ends similar (n even): optical isomers = 2^(n-1), meso = 2^((n-2)/2) If n odd: optical isomers = 2^(n-1) - 2^((n-1)/2)
(a) Enantiomers: Non-superimposable mirror images. Same physical & chemical properties except optical rotation direction.
(b) Diastereomers: Non-superimposable, NOT mirror images. Different physical AND chemical properties.
(c) Meso form: Half of molecule is mirror image of other half. Has plane of symmetry. Optically inactive (internal compensation). Cannot be resolved into d and l forms.
(d) Racemic mixture: 1:1 mixture of enantiomers. Optically inactive (external compensation). Separation = resolution (mechanical / biochemical / chemical method).
(e) Atropisomers: Isomers inter-convertible by rotation about a single bond, but rotation barrier is large enough that they can be separated and do not convert readily at room temperature.
(f) Specific Rotation:
[alpha] = alpha_observed / (l x d) = observed rotation (°) / (path length x density)
Nomenclature of Enantiomers
  • D-L Configuration: H on left side = D-form; H on right = L-form. Applicable mainly for compounds with one chiral atom.
  • Erythro-Threo system: Same groups on same side of carbon chain = erythro; same groups on opposite side = threo.
  • R-S System (Cahn-Ingold-Prelog): Priority by atomic number. Clockwise sequence = R (rectus); anti-clockwise = S (sinister).
    Priority rules: (1) Higher atomic number = higher priority (Br > Cl > F). (2) Groups compared by atomic number of first atom. (3) If first atoms same, compare second atoms. (4) Multiple bonds treated as equivalent number of single bonds.
(ii) Geometrical (cis-trans) Isomerism

Different spatial arrangement of groups about a double bond.

Conditions: (1) At least one double bond; (2) Groups at each double-bonded C must be different (one similar group must be present at adjacent C).

Number of geometrical isomers (two ends not similar) = 2^n (n = number of double bonds)
(a) Cis-trans: Similar groups same side (cis) or opposite side (trans). Cycloalkanes also exhibit this.
cis-but-2-ene: mu = 0.33 D (polar) trans-but-2-ene: mu = 0 (non-polar)
(b) Syn-anti: For C=N bond (aldoximes) and N=N bonds.
CH3CH=N-OH: syn and anti isomers
(c) E-Z system: E (entgegen = opposite): heavier groups on opposite sides; Z (Zusammen = together): heavier groups on same side.
⚡ Bond Fission & Attacking Reagents
1. Homolytic Fission
One electron goes to each atom → neutral free radicals. Occurs in non-polar covalent molecules under sunlight or high temperature.
A-B → A• + B• Cl2 → 2Cl• (sunlight)
Free radicals: highly reactive, neutral, electron deficient.
2. Heterolytic Fission
Both shared electrons go to one fragment → ions. Occurs in polar covalent molecules; non-polar molecules with AlCl3/FeCl3 (anhydrous) catalysts.
A-B → A+ + B- (electrophile + nucleophile) or A-B → A- + B+ (nucleophile + electrophile)
Carbon (+ve charge) = carbocation; Carbon (-ve charge) = carbanion.
Electrophiles (Lewis Acids)
Electron-deficient species. Attack electron-rich areas.
  • Non-metal cations & metal cations with vacant d-orbitals: Cl+, NO+, CH3CO+
  • Lewis acids (incomplete octet): BF3, ZnCl2, FeCl3, AlCl3 (all anhydrous)
  • Non-metal acidic oxides: CO2, SO2
Nucleophiles (Lewis Bases)
Electron-rich species. Attack electron-deficient areas.
  • All anions: Cl-, NH2-, OH-
  • Lewis bases: NH3, H2O, R-O-R, R-OH
  • Benzene, alkenes
Nucleophilicity: H- > CH3- > NH2- > RO- > OH-
Ambiphiles
Behave as both electrophile AND nucleophile.
e.g. H2C=O (formaldehyde): electrophilic C + nucleophilic O;
CH3C≡N (acetonitrile): electrophilic C + nucleophilic N.
🔬 Reaction Intermediates

Formed as short-lived, highly reactive intermediates during a reaction.

1. Free Radicals
Product of homolysis. Contain odd electron. Planar (sp2 hybridised).
Stability order: (C6H5)3C• > (C6H5)2CH• > C6H5CH2• > CH2=CH-CH2• > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH2=CH•
2. Carbocations
Product of heterolysis. C bears +ve charge. 6 valence electrons, sp2 (planar), empty p-orbital.
Stability: (C6H5)3C+ > (C6H5)2CH+ > (CH3)3C+ > C6H5CH2+ > 2° > CH2=CH-CH2+ > 1° > CH3+ > CH2=CH+
3. Carbanions
Product of heterolysis. C bears -ve charge. 8 valence electrons, sp3 (pyramidal), one lone pair.
Stability: (C6H5)3C- > (C6H5)2CH- > C6H5CH2- > CH2=CH-CH2- > CH3- > 1° > 2° > 3°
4. Carbenes
Divalent carbon species with two non-bonding electrons + two bond pairs. Formed by photolysis or pyrolysis. Behave as Lewis acids.
CH2=C=O (hv) → :CH2 + :C=O (ketene photolysis)
  • Singlet carbene: sp2, bent, less stable.
    Order: :CH2 > :CF2 > :CCl2 > :CBr2
  • Triplet carbene: sp, linear, more stable.
5. Nitrenes
Neutral monovalent nitrogen species with two unshared pairs on N + one mono-valent atom/group. Formed by thermolysis of azides. As reactive as carbenes.
Two types: singlet nitrene and triplet nitrene.
6. Arynes
Contains a formal C≡C triple bond in an aromatic molecule. Additional bond formed between two neighbouring sp2-C atoms by sideways overlapping of two sp2-orbitals. New bond lies along side of ring with little interaction with pi-electron cloud.
Sideways overlapping is weak → makes benzene more reactive. Most reactive intermediate in aromatic chemistry.
⚛️ Electronic Effects
Inductive Effect (I)
Shifting of shared electron pair towards more electronegative atom. Permanent effect; propagates through chain (diminishes with distance).
d+ d- CH3 -→- Cl dd+ d+ d- CH3 -→- CH2 -→- Cl
-I effect groups (electron withdrawing):
RN+3 > NO2 > CN > SO3H > CHO > CO > COOH > F > Cl > Br > I > OH > OR > NH2 > C6H5
+I effect groups (electron releasing):
O- > COO- > 3°alkyl > 2°alkyl > 1°alkyl > CH3 > H
Applications:
  • +I groups stabilise carbocations; -I groups destabilise.
  • -I groups increase acid strength; +I groups decrease it.
  • +I groups increase basic strength of amines.
  • -I groups increase reactivity of carbonyl compounds.
  • +I groups increase reactivity towards SN1.
Electromeric Effect (E)
Polarity produced in a multiple-bonded compound as reagent approaches. Two pi-electrons completely transferred to one atom. Temporary effect.
+E: electron pair displaced away from atom -E: electron pair displaced towards atom C=C + E+ reagent → C+-C- (electrophilic) C=O + Nu- reagent → C-O- (nucleophilic)
Hyperconjugation (Baker-Nathan / No-Bond Resonance)
Delocalisation of sigma-electrons of C-H bond of alkyl group adjacent to unsaturated system.
H+ + CH2-CH=CH-CH2 ↔ CH3-CH+-CH=CH2
Applications:
  • More alpha-H atoms → more stable alkene.
  • More alkyl groups on carbocation → greater stability.
(CH3)3C+ > (CH3)2CH+ > CH3CH2+ > CH3+
Resonance Effect (Mesomeric Effect)

When all properties cannot be shown by a single structure → resonating structures (canonical forms). Actual molecule = resonance hybrid.

Rules: (1) Same atomic arrangement; (2) Nearly same energy; (3) Same number of unpaired electrons. Involves delocalisation of pi-electrons.

+R Effect: Electron-donating groups w.r.t. conjugated system. Central atom more electronegative than surrounding atoms.
Groups: halogens, -OH, -OR, -OCOR, -NH2, -NHCORs
ortho & para directing; activate benzene ring (except halogens which slightly deactivate). More EDG → more basic nature.
-R Effect: Electron-withdrawing groups. Central atom less electronegative than surrounding atoms.
Groups: halogens, -COOH, -COOR, -CHO, -CN, -NO2
meta directing; deactivate benzene ring. More EWG → more acidic nature.
Stability of Canonical Forms:
  1. Non-polar structure more stable than polar.
  2. Among polar structures, maximum covalent bonds = most stable.
  3. Maximum charge separation = less stable.
  4. +ve charge on electropositive element, -ve on electronegative = more stable.
Resonance energy of benzene = 36 kcal/mol Bond order = Total bonds between two atoms / Total resonating structures e.g. benzene: BO = (2+1)/2 = 1.5 More pi-bonds → more canonical forms → higher resonance energy → greater stability
⇄ Types of Organic Reactions
1. Addition Reactions
Given by unsaturated compounds (multiple bonds). Reagent adds to substrate.
  • Electrophilic Addition (EA): H+ or electrophile adds in rate-determining step. Given by alkenes and alkynes.
    C=C + H+ → C-C+ then X- adds → C-C with H and X
  • Nucleophilic Addition (NA): Nucleophile adds in rate-determining step. Given by carbonyl compounds.
    C=O + CN- → C(O-)(CN) → C(OH)(CN)
2. Substitution Reactions
Leaving group is replaced by nucleophile or electrophile.
  • SE (Electrophilic substitution): Electrophile replaces leaving group. e.g. nitration of benzene.
  • SN1 (Unimolecular, 2-step): R-X → R+ + X-, then R+ + Nu → R-Nu.
    Rate = k[RX]. Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°.
  • SN2 (Bimolecular, 1-step): Backside attack → inversion of configuration.
    Rate = k[RX][Nu]. Reactivity: 1° > 2° > 3°.
3. Elimination Reactions
Two groups lost from same or adjacent atoms → unsaturated compound.
  • alpha-elimination: Both groups from same carbon (rare). Produces carbene.
    CHCl3 → :CCl2 (carbene) + HCl
  • beta-elimination (E1/E2): Groups from adjacent carbon atoms.
    H + X from adjacent C → alkene + HX
4. Rearrangement Reactions
Migration of atom/group within the same molecule.
Hofmann Bromamide Reaction: R-CO-NH2 + Br2/KOH → R-NH2 (1° amine) (R migrates from C to N) via: acyl nitrene → alkyl isocyanate → 1° amine
Chapter 25 — Hydrocarbons
🌿 Introduction to Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds containing carbon and hydrogen only. e.g. alkanes, alkenes, alkynes.

Classification: Hydrocarbons ├── Open Chain (Aliphatic) │ ├── Saturated → Alkanes [CnH(2n+2)] │ └── Unsaturated → Alkenes [CnH(2n)] & Alkynes [CnH(2n-2)] └── Cyclic ├── Aromatic → Benzenoids (benzene, toluene) & Non-benzenoids (tropolone) └── Alicyclic → e.g. cyclobutane
🔵 Alkanes (Paraffins)

Saturated, open-chain hydrocarbons with C-C single bonds. General formula: CnH(2n+2). All H-C-H bond angles = 109.5°. Only sigma-bonds; carbon at centre with H at corners of regular tetrahedron.

Earlier called paraffins (Latin: parum = little; affins = affinity — inert under normal conditions). Exhibit chain isomerism and position isomerism.

Methods of Preparation of Alkanes
(i) Hydrogenation of Alkenes/Alkynes:
CH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3-CH3 (Pt/Pd/Ni) CH3-C≡C-H + 2H2 → CH3-CH2-CH3 (Pt/Pd/Ni) More steric crowding at multiple bond → less reactive towards hydrogenation
(ii) Soda Lime Decarboxylation (Descending Series):
RCOONa + NaOH/CaO (Delta) → R-H + Na2CO3 Alkane has one carbon less than parent compound. CaO keeps NaOH dry (more hygroscopic).
(iii) Wurtz Reaction (increases chain length):
R-X + 2Na + X-R → R-R + 2NaX (dry ether) Used for higher alkanes only. Mixed Wurtz (different RX) gives mixture of alkanes.
(iv) Reduction of Alkyl Halides:
R-X + 2[H] → R-H + HX (Zn-Cu/alcohol) Reducing agents: Zn/HCl, HI/Red P, H2/Pd can also be used.
(v) Kolbe's Electrolysis (only even-carbon alkanes):
2CH3COONa + 2H2O → CH3-CH3 + 2NaOH + 2CO2 + H2 (electrolysis) Alkane and CO2 liberated at ANODE; H2 at CATHODE. Only even number of C-atoms can be formed.
(vi) Clemmensen's Reduction:
R-CO-R' + 4[H] → R-CH2-R' + H2O (Zn-Hg/HCl catalyst) e.g. ethanal + 4[H] → ethane + H2O
(vii) Wolff-Kishner Reduction:
R2C=O + H2N-NH2 → R2C=N-NH2 (hydrazone) → R2CH2 + N2 (KOH/glycol, 435-473 K)
(viii) From Compounds Containing Oxygen (HI/Red P):
ROH + 2HI → R-H + H2O + I2 (Red P, 150°C) R-CHO + 4HI → R-CH3 + H2O + 2I2 RCOOH + 6HI → R-CH3 + 2H2O + 3I2
(ix) From Carbides:
Al4C3 + 12H2O → 4Al(OH)3 + 3CH4 (methane) Be2C + 4H2O → 2Be(OH)2 + CH4 (methane)
(x) Corey-House Synthesis (odd number of C-atoms):
RX + 2Li → R-Li + LiX (ether) 2R-Li + CuI → LiR2Cu (lithium dialkylcuprate) LiR2Cu + R'X → R-R' + R-Cu + LiX
⚠️ Methane CANNOT be prepared by: Wurtz reaction, Kolbe's electrolytic process, or reduction of alkenes/alkynes.
Physical Properties of Alkanes
  • First four members (C1-C4): colourless gases; next 13: colourless liquids; higher members: colourless solids.
  • Explanation based on van der Waals' forces (VAF).
    BP ∝ VAF ∝ molecular mass ∝ surface area
    BP decreases with branching: n-octane > iso-octane > 2,2,3,3-tetramethylbutane
  • Alkanes with even number of C-atoms have higher melting points than adjacent odd-numbered members.
  • Non-polar → soluble in non-polar solvents; insoluble in water.
Chemical Properties of Alkanes
(i) Halogenation (Free Radical Chain Mechanism):
CH4 + Cl2 (hv) → CH3Cl → CH2Cl2 → CHCl3 → CCl4 Reactivity of halogens: F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 Reactivity of H: 3° > 2° > 1° Attacking reagent = halogen free radical (X•)
(ii) Combustion:
CnH(2n+2) + (3n+1)/2 O2 → nCO2 + (n+1)H2O Large heat evolved → used as fuels
(iii) Controlled Oxidation:
2CH4 + O2 → 2CH3OH (Cu/523K/100 atm) CH4 + O2 → HCHO + H2O (Mo2O3/Delta) 2CH3-CH3 + 3O2 → 2CH3COOH + 2H2O [(CH3COO)2Mn/Delta] CH4 + O2 (limited) → C (carbon black) + 2H2O
(iv) Isomerisation:
n-hexane → 2-methylpentane + 3-methylpentane (Anhydrous AlCl3/HCl)
(v) Aromatisation (Dehydrocyclisation):
n-hexane → benzene (Cr2O3/V2O5 or Mo2O3, 773K, 10-20 atm)
(vi) Reaction with Steam (Synthesis gas):
CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2 (Ni/Delta)
(vii) Pyrolysis/Cracking:
C12H26 → C7H16 + C5H10 + other products (Pt/Pd/Ni catalyst)
(viii) Nitration:
C6H13-H + HO-NO2 → C6H13NO2 + H2O (Delta, gives nitrohexane)
(ix) Sulphonation:
C6H13-H + conc. H2SO4 → C6H13SO3H + H2O (400°C, gives hexane sulphonic acid)

Conformations of Alkanes (Ethane)

C-C sigma-bonds allow free rotation → different spatial arrangements called conformations (conformers/rotamers).

Types (Sawhorse & Newman Projections):
  • Eclipsed form: H atoms of front and rear C aligned → maximum torsional strain → least stable.
  • Staggered form: H atoms at 60° → minimum torsional strain → most stable.
  • Gauche (skew): Intermediate between eclipsed and staggered.
Energy difference (eclipsed - staggered) = 12.5 kJ/mol Stability order: Staggered > Gauche > Eclipsed
🔶 Alkenes (Olefins)

Unsaturated non-cyclic hydrocarbons. General formula: CnH(2n). sp2-hybridised, bond angle 120°. Also called olefins (oil-forming, indicating high reactivity). C1-C3 are gases; next 14 are liquids; higher = solids. Show chain, position, and geometrical isomerism.

cis-but-2-ene: dipole moment = 0.33 D (polar); trans-but-2-ene: dipole moment = 0 (non-polar due to symmetry).
Methods of Preparation of Alkenes
(i) From Alkynes (gives cis-alkene with Lindlar's catalyst):
R-C≡C-R' + H2 → cis-RCH=CHR' (Pd/C, Lindlar's)
(ii) Dehydrohalogenation (from alkyl halides, beta-elimination):
R-CH2-CHX-R' + alc. KOH (Delta) → R-CH=CH-R' + HX
(iii) Dehalogenation (from vicinal dihalides + Zn):
CH2Br-CH2Br + Zn → CH2=CH2 + ZnBr2 CH3-CHBr-CH2Br + Zn → CH3-CH=CH2 + ZnBr2
(iv) Dehydration of Alcohols:
CH3-CH2-OH + Conc. H2SO4 (Delta) → CH2=CH2 + H2O
Chemical Properties of Alkenes
(i) Addition of Halogens:
CH2=CH2 + Br-Br (CCl4) → CH2Br-CH2Br (1,2-dibromoethane; test for unsaturation)
(ii) Addition of HX (Markownikoff's Rule):
Markownikoff's rule: negative part of addendum goes to C with fewer H atoms. CH3CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3CHBr-CH3 (2-bromopropane) Anti-Markownikoff / Kharash effect (organic peroxide): CH3CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3CH2CH2Br (1-bromopropane) Only HBr shows peroxide effect (not HCl or HI) Reactivity order of HX: HI > HBr > HCl
(iii) Addition of Cold Conc. H2SO4:
CH3CH=CH2 + HOSO3H (cold, conc.) → CH3CH(OSO3H)CH3 (propyl hydrogen sulphate)
(iv) Addition of Water (acid-catalysed hydration):
2-methylpropene + H2O (H+) → 2-methylpropan-2-ol (Markownikoff product)
(v) Oxymercuration-Demercuration (anti-addition, avoids rearrangement):
Alkene + (CH3COO)2Hg (THF/H2O) → organomercury compound NaBH4/NaOH → Markownikoff alcohol (anti-addition) Better than H2SO4 hydration: avoids rearrangement.
(vi) Hydroboration-Oxidation (syn-addition, anti-Markownikoff):
6(R-CH=CH2) + BH3 → 2(R-CH2-CH2)3B (trialkylborane) OH-/H2O2 → R-CH2-CH2-OH (primary alcohol) Reaction involves syn-addition of reagent.
(vii) Oxidation:
Baeyer's test (cold dil. KMnO4, 273K): CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O → CH2(OH)-CH2(OH) (glycol) Decolourises KMnO4 → test for unsaturation. Acidic KMnO4 or K2Cr2O7: (CH3)2C=CH2 → (CH3)2C=O + CO2 + H2O CH3CH=CHCH3 → 2CH3COOH
(viii) Ozonolysis:
C=C + O3 → [ozonide] → aldehydes/ketones (Zn/H2O) CH3-CH=CH2 + O3 → [ozonide] → CH3CHO + HCHO Zn + H2O → ZnO + H2O
(ix) Polymerisation:
n(CH2=CH2) → [-CH2-CH2-]n (polythene) n(CH3-CH=CH2) → [-CH2-CH(CH3)-]n (polypropene) (High temp/pressure + catalyst)
(x) With Sulphur Monochloride (Mustard Gas):
2CH2=CH2 + S2Cl2 → S(CH2CH2Cl)2 + S (mustard gas / war gas)
(xi) Diels-Alder Reaction (conjugated dienes):
1,3-butadiene + ethylene (dienophile) → cyclohexene Conjugated alkenes (alternate single & double bonds) give this reaction.
(xii) Substitution at Allylic Position (high temperature):
CH3-CH=CH2 + Cl2 (500°C) → ClCH2-CH=CH2 + HCl (allyl chloride / 3-chloroprop-1-ene)

🔴 Alkynes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH(2n-2). e.g. ethyne (C2H2), propyne (C3H4). sp-hybridised. H-C-C bond angle = 180°. Bond length = 120 pm. Contain 3 sigma + 2 pi bonds.

Show position, chain, functional, and ring-chain isomerism. Position of triple bond determined by ozone (O3).

Physical Properties: First two members (C2H2, C3H4) are gases; C5-C12 are liquids; higher = solids. All colourless and odourless except acetylene (slightly garlic odour due to PH3 and H2S impurities). Insoluble in water; soluble in organic solvents. MP, BP, density increase with molar mass.

Methods of Preparation of Alkynes
(i) From Calcium Carbide:
CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 (Delta) CaO + C → CaC2 + CO (Delta) CaC2 + 2H2O → Ca(OH)2 + HC≡CH (acetylene)
(ii) From Vicinal Dihalides:
H2C-CHX2 (or CH2X-CH2X) + 2KOH/alcohol (- KBr, - H2O) → HC≡CH (acetylene) then NaNH2-NaBr-NH3 → HC≡CH
(iii) From Tetrahalides:
CHBr2-CHBr2 + 2Zn (CH3OH/Delta) → HC≡CH + 2ZnBr2
Chemical Properties of Alkynes

Alkynes show electrophilic addition (less reactive than alkenes — pi-electron cloud needs more energy to displace) and also nucleophilic addition reactions.

(i) Acidic Character of Terminal Alkynes:
HC≡CH + Na → HC≡C-Na+ + ½H2 (monosodium acetylide) HC≡C-Na+ + Na → Na+C≡C-Na+ + ½H2 (disodium acetylide) CH3-C≡CH + NaNH2 → CH3-C≡C-Na+ + NH3
Acidic order: HC≡CH > CH2=CH2 > CH3-CH3 (sp > sp2 > sp3) Also: HC≡CH > CH3-C≡CH >> CH3-C≡C-CH3 Acidity of alkynes less than water. Non-terminal alkynes do NOT show acidic character.
(ii) Electrophilic Addition:
(a) H2: CH3-C≡CH + H2 (Pt) → propene → propane (b) Cl2: HC≡CH + Cl2 → CHCl=CHCl (1,2-dichloroethene) + Cl2 → CHCl2-CHCl2 (westron, 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane) -HCl → CH=CCl2/Cl (westrosol, trichloroethene) (c) HBr (Markownikoff): CH3-C≡CH + HBr → CH3-CBr=CH2 (2-bromopropene) + HBr → CH3-CBr2-CH3 (2,2-dibromopropane)
(iii) Addition of Water (Hg2+, H+, 333K):
CH3-C≡CH + HOH → CH3-C(OH)=CH2 (vinyl alcohol, unstable) → isomerisation → CH3-CO-CH3 (propanone/acetone) HC≡CH + HOH → CH3CHO (acetaldehyde, via vinyl alcohol)
(iv) Cyclic Polymerisation (Berthelot Synthesis):
3HC≡CH → benzene (red-hot iron tube, 873K)
(v) Reaction with AsCl3 (Lewisite):
HC≡CH + AsCl3 (Anhy. AlCl3) → CHCl=CH-AsCl2 (Lewisite, poisonous war gas)
(vi) Oxidation:
HC≡CH + 4[O] (Alk. KMnO4) → 2 x HCOOH (formic acid) CH3-C≡CH (acidic KMnO4) → CH3COOH (acetic acid) R-C≡C-R (Alk. KMnO4) → 2 x RCOOH
(vii) Ozonolysis:
HC≡CH + O3 (CCl4) → CHO-CHO (glyoxal) + Zn/H2O → 2HCOOH (formic acid) Higher alkynes → diketones → carboxylic acids
(viii) Linear Polymerisation:
2HC≡CH → CH2=CH-C≡CH [Cu2Cl2/NH4Cl] (vinylacetylene) 3HC≡CH → CH2=CH-C≡C-CH=CH2 (divinylacetylene)

⬡ Benzene & Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Parent member of aromatic hydrocarbons. Molecular formula: C6H6. Hexagonal ring of six C-atoms with three alternate double bonds (Kekule structure). Resonance-stabilised.

Physical Properties: Non-polar molecules. Usually colourless liquids or solids with characteristic aroma. Immiscible with water; readily miscible with organic solvents. Burns with sooty flame (high C:H ratio).

Aromaticity (Huckel Rule)
An aromatic compound must possess:
  1. Planarity.
  2. Complete delocalisation of pi-electrons in the ring.
  3. Presence of (4n + 2) pi-electrons in the ring where n = 0, 1, 2, ... (Huckel rule).
n=0: 2 pi electrons (cyclopropenyl cation) n=1: 6 pi electrons (benzene) n=2: 10 pi electrons (naphthalene, azulene)
Methods of Preparation of Benzene
(i) Cyclic Polymerisation of Ethyne:
3HC≡CH → C6H6 (red-hot iron tube, 873K)
(ii) Decarboxylation of Aromatic Acids:
C6H5COONa + NaOH/CaO (Delta) → C6H6 + Na2CO3
(iii) Reduction of Phenol:
C6H5OH + Zn (Delta) → C6H6 + ZnO
Electrophilic Substitution Reactions of Benzene

Benzene preferentially undergoes electrophilic substitution (maintains aromaticity). Steps: (a) Generation of electrophile; (b) Formation of carbocation arenium ion; (c) Removal of proton.

(i) Nitration:
C6H6 + HNO3 (conc.) + H2SO4 (conc.) → C6H5NO2 + H2O (323-333K)
(ii) Halogenation (Friedel-Crafts):
C6H6 + Cl2 (Anhy. AlCl3) → C6H5Cl + HCl Excess Cl2 (Anhy. AlCl3, dark, cold) → C6Cl6 (hexachlorobenzene)
(iii) Sulphonation:
C6H6 + H2SO4 (fuming, Delta) → C6H5SO3H + H2O (benzene sulphonic acid)
(iv) Friedel-Crafts Alkylation:
C6H6 + CH3Cl (Anhy. AlCl3) → C6H5CH3 + HCl (toluene) n-propyl chloride → isopropyl benzene (rearrangement: 1° → 2° carbocation)
(v) Friedel-Crafts Acylation:
C6H6 + CH3COCl (Anhy. AlCl3/Delta) or (CH3CO)2O → C6H5COCH3 + HCl (acetophenone)
Addition Reactions of Benzene:
(i) C6H6 + 3H2 (Ni/Delta) → C6H12 (cyclohexane) (ii) C6H6 + 3Cl2 (UV, 500K) → C6H6Cl6 (BHC/Gammexane/Lindane) Combustion: 2C6H6 + 15O2 → 12CO2 + 6H2O

Directive Influence of Substituents on Benzene Ring

First substituent directs the next incoming electrophile. Electrophile always attacks positions of high electron density.

ortho & para directing (activate ring): -NH2, -OH, -OR, -R (alkyl), -NHCOR, -OCOR (All show +R / +I effect) Exception: Halogens are o/p directing BUT deactivate ring
meta directing (deactivate ring): -F, -Cl, -CN, -NO2, -COOH, -CHO, -SO3H, -COOR (All show -R / -I effect) Attack at meta position (lower e- density at o/p)
☠️ Carcinogenicity & Toxicity

Benzene and polynuclear hydrocarbons containing more than two benzene rings fused together are toxic and said to possess cancer-producing (carcinogenic) properties.

1,2-Benzanthracene — polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbon; carcinogenic (cancer-causing compound).
3-Methylcholanthrene — one of the most potent chemical carcinogens known.
Toxicity of these compounds is due to their ability to form reactive metabolites (epoxides) that bind to DNA, causing mutations and cancer.

Contains mainly: alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatic hydrocarbons, sulphur, nitrogen and oxygen compounds.

Mixture of butane + iso-butane (+ small amount of propane).

📊 Chromatographic Methods

Discovered by Tswett (1906). Separation based on selective adsorption between stationary and mobile phases.

TypeStationary phaseMobile phaseTechnique
Adsorption chromatographySolid or ion exchange resinLiquid or gasColumn, thin layer, gas chromatography
Partition chromatographyLiquid on inert solidLiquid or gasLiquid-liquid or liquid-gas partition
Column Chromatography: Adsorbents (alumina, silica gel, charcoal, kieselguhr); solvents (benzene, petroleum ether, alcohol). Components separate into zones/bands by preferential adsorption.
Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC): Thin adsorbent layer (~0.2 mm) on glass plate. Mixture applied as spot 2 cm from edge. Solvent drawn up by capillary; separation by differential adsorption.
Retardation Factor (R_f): R_f = distance moved by substance (x) / distance moved by solvent front (y) 0 ≤ R_f ≤ 1; characteristic of each component
Paper Chromatography: Filter paper strip acts as stationary phase. Solutes migrate at different rates via capillary action. R_f ratio used for component identification.
Elution: Continuous pouring of solvent from column top. Weakly adsorbed components elute first (least polar solvent); strongly adsorbed later (more polar solvent).

🔬 Qualitative Analysis of Organic Compounds
Detection of C & H: Heat organic compound with dry CuO in hard glass tube.
C + 2CuO → CO + 2Cu (upon heating) CO₂ turns lime water milky 2H + CuO → H₂O + Cu (upon heating)
Detection of Nitrogen: Lassaigne test. Heat compound with sodium metal; product contains Na cyanide/nitride. Acidify with HCl and add FeSO₄ + K₃[Fe(CN)₆] → Prussian blue precipitate (N present).
Detection of Halogens: Burn compound with Na metal. Product contains NaX. Add AgNO₃ → AgX ppt. White ppt (AgCl), cream (AgBr), yellow (AgI).
Detection of Sulphur: Lassaigne test with Na. Acidify; add lead acetate → black PbS ppt.

Solubility Rules (important for qualitative analysis):
  • All common inorganic acids, Group IA metals (Na, K), and NH₄⁺ compounds are soluble.
  • All common nitrates (NO₃⁻), acetates, and perchlorates are soluble.
  • Chlorides soluble except AgCl, HgCl₂, PbCl₂.
  • Sulphates soluble except PbSO₄, BaSO₄ (slightly soluble: CaSO₄, Ag₂SO₄).
  • Hydroxides insoluble except Group IA and Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂.
  • Carbonates, phosphates insoluble except Group IA and MgCO₃ (fairly soluble).
  • Sulphides insoluble except Group IA, IIA, and (NH₄)₂S.
⚗️ Dry Tests
Borax bead test: Borax [Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O] heated on platinum loop forms transparent colourless glass-like bead.
Na₂B₄O₇ → 2NaBO₂ + B₂O₃ (on heating) Bead colour indicates metal ions: blue (Cu²⁺), green (Cr³⁺), etc.
Flame test: Direct method to detect alkali metals (Na, K, Ca, etc.) by characteristic flame colours.
Charcoal cavity test: Heat salt in charcoal cavity; observe colour, smell, nature of residue, etc. to infer metal identity.
