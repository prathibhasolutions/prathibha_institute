💧 Types of Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components. The component in excess is solvent; the other is solute.

Based on amount of solute: Unsaturated (can dissolve more) → Saturated (maximum solute at given T) → Supersaturated (unstable, more than saturated; forms by cooling or evaporation).

☀️ Solubility & Henry's Law
Henry's Law
p = KH × x where: p = partial pressure of gas x = mole fraction of gas in solution KH = Henry's law constant Higher KH → lower solubility KH increases with temperature → solubility decreases with ↑T

Applications:

  • CO₂ dissolved in soft drinks under high pressure
  • Deep-sea divers use He-O₂ mixture (N₂ causes narcosis/"bends")
  • Anoxia at high altitude (low pO₂)
Solubility of Solids in Liquids
  • "Like dissolves like" — polar solvent dissolves polar/ionic solutes
  • Temperature effect depends on dissolution enthalpy: if ΔHsol > 0 (endothermic) → solubility ↑ with T
  • Most ionic solids — solubility increases with T
  • CaCl₂, CeSO₄, Li₂CO₃ — solubility decreases with T
Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law for volatile solutes, where KH = p° (vapour pressure of pure solvent).
🌊 Raoult's Law & Vapour Pressure
Raoult's Law: pA = p°A × χA; pB = p°B × χB pTotal = pA + pB = p°A·χA + p°B·χB
Ideal Solutions
  • Obey Raoult's law at all compositions and temperatures.
  • ΔHmix = 0; ΔVmix = 0
  • A-B interactions ≈ A-A ≈ B-B

Examples:

  • Benzene + Toluene
  • n-Hexane + n-Heptane
  • Ethyl bromide + Ethyl iodide
  • Chlorobenzene + Bromobenzene

📊 Colligative Properties
Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles, not on their nature. They are applicable to dilute solutions.
1. Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure (RLVP)
p° − p n W/M ——————— = χsolute = ———— = —————— p° n + N W₀/M₀ For finding molecular mass: M = (W × M₀ × p°) / (W₀ × (p° − p)) where: W=mass of solute, W₀=mass of solvent, M=mol.wt of solute, M₀=mol.wt of solvent
Determined by Ostwald-Walker dynamic method.
2. Elevation of Boiling Point (ΔTb)
ΔTb = Kb × m where: m = molality; Kb = ebullioscopic constant Kb = 1000 × R × Tb°² / (ΔHvap × M₀) Finding mol. mass: M = (Kb × W × 1000) / (ΔTb × W₀) Kb (water) = 0.52 K kg mol⁻¹ Kb (benzene) = 2.53 K kg mol⁻¹
Determined by Beckmann method or Cottrell's method.
3. Depression of Freezing Point (ΔTf)
ΔTf = Kf × m where: m = molality; Kf = cryoscopic constant Kf = 1000 × R × Tf°² / (ΔHfus × M₀) Finding mol. mass: M = (Kf × W × 1000) / (ΔTf × W₀) Kf (water) = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹ Kf (benzene) = 5.12 K kg mol⁻¹ Kf (camphor) = 40 K kg mol⁻¹ (Rast method)
Applications: antifreeze (ethylene glycol) in car radiators; NaCl/CaCl₂ to melt snow on roads.
4. Osmotic Pressure (π)
π = CRT = (n/V)RT Finding mol. mass: M = WRT / πV where: W = mass of solute, V = volume of solution π = dRT/M (d = density, M = molar mass)
  • Semipermeable membranes: natural (animal bladder, cell membrane), artificial (Cu₂[Fe(CN)₆])
  • Hypertonic: π(sol) > π(cell) → cell shrinks (plasmolysis)
  • Hypotonic: π(sol) < π(cell) → cell bursts (haemolysis)
  • Isotonic: π(sol) = π(cell); 0.9% NaCl = isotonic with blood
  • Best method for determining polymer molecular masses.
  • Reverse osmosis: applied P > π → solvent flows out (desalination).
⚠️ Abnormal Molecular Mass & van't Hoff Factor

Solutes that dissociate or associate in solution give abnormal colligative properties.

van't Hoff factor (i): i = Observed colligative property / Calculated colligative property i = Normal molecular mass / Observed molecular mass i = Number of particles after / Number of particles before i > 1 → dissociation (electrolytes) i < 1 → association (e.g. acetic acid in benzene) i = 1 → no dissociation/association (non-electrolytes)
Dissociation: A → nP (degree of dissociation = α) i = 1 + α(n − 1) ∴ α = (i − 1) / (n − 1) Example: NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ (n=2) i = 1 + α(2−1) = 1 + α
Association: nA → Aₙ (degree of association = α) i = 1 − α(1 − 1/n) ∴ α = (1 − i) / (1 − 1/n) Example: CH₃COOH in benzene (dimer, n=2) i = 1 − α/2
