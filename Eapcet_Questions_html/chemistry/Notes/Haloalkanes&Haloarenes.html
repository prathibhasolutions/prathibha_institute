Chemistry Handbook — Chapter 26

Haloalkanes & Haloarenes

Complete Notes · Arihant Handbook of Chemistry

🧪 Introduction & Classification

The replacement of hydrogen atom(s) in a hydrocarbon by halogen atom(s) results in the formation of alkyl halide (haloalkane) and aryl halide (haloarene) respectively.

Classification by Number of Halogen Atoms
TypeExample
MonohaloalkaneCH3X, C2H5X
MonohaloareneC6H5X
Dihaloalkane (geminal or vicinal)CH2X–CH2X
TrihaloalkaneCH2X–CHX–CH2X
Classification by Nature of Carbon (C–X bond)
TypeC–X carbonExample
1° (Primary)sp3, bonded to 1 CCH3CH2Cl
2° (Secondary)sp3, bonded to 2 C(CH3)2CHCl
3° (Tertiary)sp3, bonded to 3 C(CH3)3CCl
Allylicsp3, next to C=CCH2=CH–CH2Cl
Benzylicsp3, next to benzene ringC6H5–CH2Cl
Vinylicsp2, on C=CCH2=CHCl
Arylsp2, on benzene ringC6H5Cl
Nature of C–X Bond

Due to high electronegativity of the halogen (X) atom, the C–X bond is polar (δ+C–Xδ–). The bond of haloarene is less polar than that of haloalkanes (due to resonance in aryl halides giving partial double bond character to C–X).

Bond polarity: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I (electronegativity order) Bond strength: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I (decreases with size of halogen) Bond length: C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I (increases with size of halogen)
⚗️ General Methods of Preparation of Haloalkanes
1. From Alcohols
(a) Groove's Process (Lucas Reagent = Anhy. ZnCl₂ + Conc. HCl):
R–OH + HCl + Anhy. ZnCl2 → R–Cl + H2O (Groove's process) ZnCl2 weakens the C–OH bond. For 3° alcohols, ZnCl2 is NOT required.
Reactivity of HX: HI > HBr > HCl
(b) With Phosphorus Halides:
R–OH + PCl5 → R–Cl + POCl3 + HCl 3R–OH + PBr3 → 3R–Br + H3PO3 3R–OH + PI3 → 3R–I + H3PO3
(c) Darzen Procedure (with SOCl₂ — BEST method):
R–OH + SOCl2 (Pyridine) → R–Cl + SO2↑ + HCl↑ Both byproducts (SO2 & HCl) are gaseous and escape easily. This gives the purest alkyl halide.
(d) With NaBr/H₂SO₄:
R–OH + NaBr + H2SO4 → R–Br + H2O (reflux)
2. Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes
CH3CH2CH2CH3 + Cl2 → CH3CH2CH2CH2Cl (1-chlorobutane, minor) + CH3CH2CHClCH3 (2-chlorobutane, major) (UV light or heat) Reactivity of C–H: 3° > 2° > 1° Selectivity: F2 (least selective) < Cl2 < Br2 (most selective)
3. Addition of Hydrogen Halides to Alkenes & Alkynes
Markownikoff addition (from alkenes):
CH3–CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3–CHBr–CH3 (2-bromopropane, major — Markownikoff)
Anti-Markownikoff (organic peroxide / Kharash effect):
CH3–CH=CH2 + HBr (organic peroxide) → CH3–CH2–CH2Br (1-bromopropane, major — anti-Markownikoff) Only HBr shows peroxide effect (not HCl or HI)
From Alkynes:
CH3–C≡CH + HBr → CH3–CBr=CH2 (2-bromopropene) + HBr → CH3–CBr2–CH3 (2,2-dibromopropane)
4. Special Reactions for Haloalkane Preparation
Finkelstein Reaction:
R–Cl + NaI → R–I + NaCl (acetone) R–Br + NaI → R–I + NaBr (acetone) (NaCl/NaBr precipitate drives forward)
Swarts Reaction:
CH3–Br + AgF → CH3–F + AgBr Also: Hg2F2, CoF2, SbF3 can be used (to prepare alkyl fluorides)
Hunsdiecker Reaction:
CH3COOAg + Br2 → CH3Br + AgBr + CO2 (in CCl4) RCOOAg + Br2 → R–Br + AgBr + CO2 (descends chain by one C atom)
📊 Physical Properties of Haloalkanes
Boiling Point Orders
(i) R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F (same alkyl group) (↑ molar mass → ↑ van der Waals forces → ↑ BP) (ii) CH3(CH2)2CH2Br > (CH3)2CHCH2Br > (CH3)3CBr (↑ branching → ↓ surface area → ↓ BP) (iii) CH3CH2CH2X > CH3CH2X > CH3X (↑ chain length → ↑ BP)
Other Physical Properties
  • Bond strength decreases with size of halogen:
    CH3F > CH3Cl > CH3Br > CH3I
  • Dipole moment decreases as electronegativity of halogen decreases:
    C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I
  • Density order (same alkyl group):
    RI > RBr > RCl > RF
    CH3I > C2H5I > C3H7I
  • Haloalkanes though polar are insoluble in water (do not form H-bonds with water).
⇄ Chemical Reactions of Haloalkanes
A. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions (SN)
General: Nu:⁻ + δ+C–Xδ– → C–Nu + X⁻

Common SN Reactions of C₂H₅Br (Ethyl Bromide):

Ambident Nucleophiles: KCN (ionic) provides CN⁻ → attacks via carbon → nitrile (major). AgCN (covalent) → attacks via nitrogen → isocyanide (major). Similarly, KNO2 gives nitrite (O-attack), AgNO2 gives nitroalkane (N-attack).
Vinyl chloride (CH2=CHCl) is less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution due to resonance (partial double bond character of C–Cl bond, stabilisation by electron delocalisation).
SN1 — Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution (2-step)
Step 1 (SLOW, rate-determining): (CH3)3C–X → (CH3)3C⁺ + X⁻ (carbocation) Step 2 (FAST): (CH3)3C⁺ + Nu⁻ → (CH3)3C–Nu
  • Rate = k[RX] (1st order)
  • Depends on stability of carbocation formed.
  • Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°
  • Favoured by: polar solvents, low [Nu], weak nucleophiles.
  • Gives partial racemisation — planar carbocation attacked from both sides (frontal + backside).
SN1 relative rates: (CH3)3CX > C6H5CH2X > CH2=CHCH2X > (CH3)2CHX > CH3CH2X > CH3X
SN2 — Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution (1-step)
Nu⁻ + R–X → [Nu---C---X]‡ → Nu–C + X⁻ (transition state — backside attack)
  • Rate = k[RX][Nu] (2nd order)
  • Proceeds with inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). d-halide → l-product and vice versa.
  • Reactivity: 1° > 2° > 3° (steric hindrance)
  • Favoured by: non-polar solvents, strong nucleophiles, high [Nu].
Nucleophilicity order: CN⁻ > I⁻ > OR⁻ > OH⁻ > CH3COO⁻ > H2O > F⁻ SN2 relative rates: CH3CH2X > CH2=CHCH2X > CH3X > CH3CH2X > (CH3)2CHX > (CH3)3CX
Relative reactivity for same alkyl group: RI > RBr > RCl > RF (for both SN1 and SN2).
Resonance-stabilised carbocations: Benzyl (C6H5CH2+) and allyl (CH2=CH–CH2+) are more stable → react faster in SN1.
B. Elimination Reactions (Dehydrohalogenation)

Beta-elimination — halogen lost from α-carbon; H lost from β-carbon. Follows Saytzeff Rule: more substituted (stable) alkene is the major product.

CH3–CH2–CHBr–CH3 + alc. KOH, Δ (2-bromobutane) → CH3–CH=CH–CH3 (but-2-ene, MAJOR — Saytzeff product) + CH3–CH2–CH=CH2 (but-1-ene, minor)
Ease of dehydrohalogenation: 3° > 2° > 1° i.e. (CH3)3CCl > (CH3)2CHCl > CH3CH2Cl
C. Reduction
C2H5Br + H2 (Ni, 575K) → C2H6 + HBr C2H5I + HI (Red P, 420K) → C2H6 + I2
D. Reaction with Metals
(i) Wurtz Reaction:
R–X + 2Na + X–R → R–R + 2NaX (dry ether) e.g. 2C2H5Br + 2Na → C4H10 + 2NaBr (butane)
(ii) Reaction with Mg — Grignard Reagent:
C2H5Br + Mg → C2H5–Mg–Br (dry ether) (Grignard's reagent — ethyl magnesium bromide) ⚠️ Grignard reagent is NEVER isolated in solid state (explodes when dry). Always used in ethereal solution.
E. Isomerisation
CH3CH2CH2Cl → CH3–CHCl–CH3 (1-chloropropane) (573K, anhy. AlCl3) (2-chloropropane)
⚗️ General Methods of Preparation of Haloarenes (Aryl Halides)
1. Halogenation of Aromatic Hydrocarbons (EAS)
C6H6 + Cl2 (FeCl3, dark, 310–320K) → C6H5Cl + HCl (chlorobenzene) Electrophilic substitution reaction.
2. Side Chain Halogenation (Free Radical)
C6H5CH3 + Cl2 (sunlight) → C6H5CH2Cl (benzyl chloride) + Cl2 (sunlight) → C6H5CHCl2 (benzal chloride) + Cl2 (sunlight) → C6H5CCl3 (benzotrichloride) Free radical mechanism (side chain substitution)
3. From Benzene Diazonium Salt
Diazonium salt → Chlorobenzene: C6H5N2+Cl⁻ + CuCl/HCl → C6H5Cl + N2 (Sandmeyer) C6H5N2+Cl⁻ + Cu/HCl → C6H5Cl + N2 (Gattermann) → Bromobenzene: + CuBr/HBr → C6H5Br + N2 (Sandmeyer) + Cu/HBr → C6H5Br + N2 (Gattermann) → Fluorobenzene (Balz-Schiemann): C6H5N2+Cl⁻ + HBF4 (273K) → C6H5N2+BF4⁻ Δ, –N2, –BF3 → C6H5F → Iodobenzene: C6H5N2+Cl⁻ + KI, Δ → C6H5I + N2 + KCl
4. From Phenol
C6H5OH + PCl5 → C6H5Cl + HCl + POCl3 (chlorobenzene)
📊 Physical Properties of Haloarenes
  • Aryl halides are colourless liquids or colourless solids with characteristic odour.
  • Boiling point generally increases with increase in size of aryl group or halogen atom:
    Ar–I > Ar–Br > Ar–Cl > Ar–F
  • Melting point of para-isomer is more than ortho and meta isomers, because para-isomer is more symmetrical (fits better in crystal lattice).
  • Due to resonance in chlorobenzene, C–Cl bond has partial double bond character → C–Cl bond is shorter and dipole moment is less than that of cyclohexyl chloride.
⇄ Chemical Properties of Haloarenes
1. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

Aryl halides are less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution reactions. Reasons:

  1. Due to resonance, C–X bond has partial double bond character → harder to break.
  2. Stabilisation of the molecule by delocalisation of electrons.
  3. Instability of phenyl carbocation (sp2 C cannot easily hold +ve charge).
C6H5Cl + NaOH (623K, 300 atm) → C6H5ONa → C6H5OH (phenol) (only under drastic conditions — Dow process)
Effect of electron-withdrawing groups: Presence of –NO2, –SO3H, etc. at ortho and para positions increases the reactivity of aryl halides towards nucleophilic substitution (stabilise the carbanion intermediate).
2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene + NaOH (443K) → 2,4-dinitrophenol (much more reactive than plain chlorobenzene) 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene + H2O (warm) → 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid — very reactive)
2. Electrophilic Substitution Reactions

Halogens are deactivating but ortho/para-directing (–I deactivates ring; +R directs to o/p). Thus, halogenation, nitration, sulphonation, and Friedel-Crafts reactions give mixtures of ortho and para substituted products.

(i) Halogenation:
C6H5Cl + Cl2 (Anhy. FeCl3) → 1,2-dichlorobenzene (minor) + 1,4-dichlorobenzene (major)
(ii) Nitration:
C6H5Cl + Conc. HNO3 + Conc. H2SO4 → 1-chloro-2-nitrobenzene (minor) + 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene (major)
(iii) Sulphonation:
C6H5Cl + Conc. H2SO4, Δ → 2-chlorobenzene sulphonic acid (minor) + 4-chlorobenzene sulphonic acid (major)
(iv) Friedel-Crafts Alkylation:
C6H5Cl + CH3Cl (Anhy. AlCl3) → 1-chloro-2-methylbenzene (minor) + 1-chloro-4-methylbenzene (major)
(v) Friedel-Crafts Acylation:
C6H5Cl + CH3COCl (Anhy. AlCl3) → 2-chloroacetophenone (minor) + 4-chloroacetophenone (major)
3. Reaction with Metals
(i) Wurtz-Fittig Reaction (aryl + alkyl halide + Na):
C6H5X + 2Na + RX → C6H5–R + 2NaX (dry ether) e.g. C6H5Br + 2Na + CH3Br → C6H5CH3 + 2NaBr (toluene)
(ii) Fittig Reaction (two aryl halides + Na):
2C6H5X + 2Na → C6H5–C6H5 + 2NaX (dry ether) (diphenyl / biphenyl)
(iii) Ullmann Reaction (Cu powder):
2C6H5I + Cu powder, Δ → C6H5–C6H5 + CuI2 (biphenyl)

⚠️ Trihalogen Derivatives
1. Chloroform (Trichloromethane, CHCl₃)
Methods of Preparation
(i) Chlorination of methane:
CH4 + 3Cl2 (sunlight, controlled) → CHCl3 + 3HCl
(ii) Haloform Reaction:
CH3CH2OH + Cl2 → CH3CHO + 2HCl (oxidation) CH3CHO + 3Cl2 → CCl3CHO + 3HCl (chlorination) 2CCl3CHO + Ca(OH)2 → 2CHCl3 + (HCOO)2Ca (hydrolysis)
Important Chemical Properties
(i) Oxidation → Phosgene (poisonous!):
2CHCl3 + O2 (light) → 2COCl2 + 2HCl (phosgene — deadly poison) ⚠️ Stored in dark brown bottles filled to brim. 1% ethanol converts COCl2 → diethyl carbonate (safe).
(ii) Production of Freon R-22:
CHCl3 + 2HF → CHClF2 + 2HCl (Freon R-22, refrigerant)
(iii) Nitration → Chloropicrin (tear-producing insecticide):
CHCl3 + HO–NO2 (conc.) → NO2–CCl3 + H2O (chloropicrin)
(iv) Dehalogenation → Acetylene:
CHCl3 + 6Ag + CHCl3 → CH≡CH + 6AgCl (Δ)
(v) Hydrolysis → Formate:
CHCl3 + 3NaOH → HC(OH)3 → HCOONa (sodium formate) (–2H2O)
2. Iodoform (Tri-iodomethane, CHI₃)

Prepared by the Iodoform Reaction:

CH3COCH3 + 3I2 + 4NaOH → CHI3↓ + 3NaI + CH3COONa + 3H2O (acetone) (iodoform — yellow crystals)
Compounds containing CH3CO– group (methyl ketones) or CH3CH(OH)– group (secondary alcohols with adjacent –CH3) give yellow-coloured iodoform precipitate with I2 and NaOH.
This is the Iodoform test for detection of these groups.
Iodoform decomposes easily to free iodine (antiseptic). Due to its objectionable smell, it has been replaced by other iodine formulations.
🧪 Polyhalogen Derivatives
1. Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl₄ / Tetrachloromethane)
Preparation:
CH4 + 4Cl2 (sunlight, hv) → CCl4 + 4HCl CHCl3 + Cl2 (hv) → CCl4 + 2HCl

Properties: Colourless, non-inflammable, poisonous liquid; soluble in alcohol and ether.

Uses:
  • Solvent for oils, fats, resins
  • Dry cleaning
  • Fire extinguisher — sold as 'Pyrene'
2. Freons (Chlorofluorocarbons — CFCs)

Chlorofluorocarbon compounds of methane and ethane collectively called freons. Produced for aerosol propellants, refrigeration, and air conditioning.

CCl4 + SbF3 (SbCl5 catalyst) → CCl2F2 + SbCl3 (dichlorodifluoromethane — Freon-12) CCl3H + 2HF → CClF2H + 2HCl (Freon R-22)
CFCs deplete the ozone layer (release Cl• radicals in stratosphere).
3. DDT (p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane)
Full name: 2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)-1,1,1-trichloroethane Structure: Cl–C6H4–CHCCl3–C6H4–Cl (two p-chlorophenyl groups + CCl3 group)
  • First chlorinated organic insecticide.
  • Prepared from chloral + chlorobenzene + conc. H2SO4.
  • Its stability and fat solubility is a major problem (bioaccumulation → carcinogenic).
4. Perchloroethane (C₂Cl₆)
  • Used as moth repellant.
  • Also known as artificial camphor.
