Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Chemistry & Matter

Chemistry is the branch of science which deals with the composition, structure, and properties of matter. Antoine Laurent Lavoisier is called the father of chemistry.

Branches of Chemistry
  • Inorganic Chemistry: Study of elements (other than carbon) and their compounds.
  • Organic Chemistry: Concerned with hydrides of carbon and their derivatives (originally substances from living organisms).
  • Physical Chemistry: Explanation of fundamental principles governing chemical phenomena.
  • Analytical Chemistry: Qualitative and quantitative analysis of chemical substances.
  • Others: Biochemistry, Nuclear chemistry, Forensic chemistry, Earth chemistry, etc.
Matter & Its Classification

Matter is anything which occupies space and has mass. It is made of small particles with spaces between them, continuously in motion.

Mixture Separation Methods
  • Filtration: Separates suspended solids from liquids through a filter.
  • Distillation: Heating liquid → vapour → cooling back to liquid; separates volatile mixtures.
  • Sublimation: Solid → vapour on heating (e.g. iodine, naphthalene, camphor).
  • Crystallisation: Separates solids with different solubilities in a solvent.
  • Magnetic separation: Separates magnetic from non-magnetic components.
  • Atmolysis: Based on rates of diffusion; separates gaseous mixtures.

Atoms, Molecules & Physical Quantities

  • Atom: Smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction; may or may not exist independently.
  • Molecule: Simplest particle of matter with independent existence. Can be homoatomic (H₂, Cl₂, O₃) or heteroatomic (HCl, NH₃, CH₄).

Some Physical Quantities
  • Mass: Constant at all places; SI unit = kg (lab unit = gram).
  • Weight: Force exerted by gravity; varies with location; unit = Newton (N).
  • Temperature conversions: \[°F = \frac{9}{5}(°C) + 32 \qquad K = °C + 273.15\]
  • Volume: Space occupied by matter; SI unit = m³.
  • Density: Mass per unit volume; units = kg m⁻³ or g cm⁻³.

Scientific Notation, Significant Figures & Dimensional Analysis

Scientific Notation

All measurements expressed as \(N \times 10^n\), where \(N\) = digit term (1.000–9.999) and \(n\) = exponent.

e.g. 138.42 cm = \(1.3842 \times 10^2\)  |  0.0002 = \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\)

Precision & Accuracy
  • Precision: Closeness of values from identical measurements (reproducibility).
    Precision = individual value − arithmetic mean value
  • Accuracy: Difference between experimental/mean value and true value.
    Accuracy = mean value − true value
  • Accurate results are generally precise, but precise results need not be accurate.
Significant Figures

Meaningful digits in a measured quantity — all known digits plus one uncertain digit. Greater significant figures → smaller uncertainty.

Rules for counting significant figures:

  1. All non-zero digits and zeros between them are significant.
  2. Zeros to the right of the decimal point are significant (e.g. 15.0 has 3 sig figs).
  3. Leading zeros (e.g. 0.0132) are NOT significant — 0.132, 0.0132, and 15.0 all have 3 sig figs.
  4. Exact numbers have infinite significant figures.

Calculations:

  • Addition/Subtraction: Report to the same number of decimal places as the term with the least decimal places.
  • Multiplication/Division: Report to the same number of significant figures as the least precise term.

Rounding off: If following digit ≥ 5, round up last retained digit; if ≤ 4, leave as is.

Dimensional Analysis (Unit Factor Method)

Method for converting units using conversion factors:

Information sought = Information given × Conversion factor

Important conversion factors:

  • 1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³ = 10⁻³ m³ = 1 dm³

  • 1 J = 10⁷ erg  |  1 L·atm = 101.325 J

Laws of Chemical Combinations

Six Fundamental Laws
  • Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1789): Total mass of products = total mass of reactants in any physical or chemical change. Does not hold for nuclear reactions.
  • Law of Definite Proportions (Proust, 1799): A chemical compound always contains the same percentage of each constituent element regardless of source.
  • Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton, 1803): When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers. e.g. in NH₃ and N₂H₄, H combines in ratio 3:2.
  • Law of Reciprocal Proportions (Richter, 1792): When elements A and B combine separately with the same weight of a third element C, the ratio in which they do so is the same or a simple multiple of the ratio in which A and B combine with each other.
  • Gay Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808): Under similar T and P, volumes of reacting gases and gaseous products bear simple whole number ratios.
  • Avogadro's Hypothesis: Equal volumes of all gases under the same conditions of T and P contain the same number of molecules.

Note: Laws of definite, multiple, and reciprocal proportions do not hold when the same compound is formed using different isotopes (e.g. H₂O and D₂O).

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803)

Postulates:

  1. All substances are made of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms.
  2. Atoms of the same element are alike and have the same mass; atoms of different elements differ in mass.
  3. Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed during any physical or chemical change.
  4. Compounds result from the combination of atoms in simple numerical ratios.

Limitations: Could not explain how atoms combine, nor the differences in masses, sizes, and valencies of different elements.

Atomic Mass, Molecular Mass & Equivalent Mass

Atomic Mass

Average relative mass of an atom; indicates how many times an atom is heavier than \(\tfrac{1}{12}\) of the mass of a C-12 atom.

\[\text{Average atomic mass} = \frac{\text{RA}(1) \times \text{at. mass}(1) + \text{RA}(2) \times \text{at. mass}(2)}{\text{RA}(1) + \text{RA}(2)}\]

where RA = relative abundance of isotopes.

  • Dulong & Petit's rule: Atomic weight × specific heat ≈ 6.4
  • For volatile chlorides: At. wt. = Eq. wt. × valency; valency = \(\dfrac{2 \times \text{VD of chloride}}{\text{eq. wt. of metal} + 35.5}\)
  • Gram Atomic Mass (GAM): Atomic mass expressed in grams = 1 gram-atom = 1 mole-atom.
Molecular Mass & Formula Mass
  • Molecular Mass: Number of times a molecule is heavier than \(\tfrac{1}{12}\) of C-12 atom. Sum of atomic masses of all atoms in the molecule.
  • Molecular mass = 2 × Vapour Density (VD)
  • Gram Molecular Mass (Molar Mass): Molecular mass expressed in grams.
  • Formula Mass: Used for substances that don't exist as discrete molecules (e.g. NaCl = 58.5 u).
Equivalent Mass & n-factor

Mass that combines with or displaces 1 part H, 8 parts O, or 35.5 parts Cl.

\[\text{Eq. wt. of metal} = \frac{\text{wt. of metal}}{\text{wt. of H}_2 \text{ displaced}} \times 1.008 = \frac{\text{wt.}}{\text{wt. of O combined}} \times 8\] \[\text{Equivalent mass} = \frac{\text{Molecular mass}}{n\text{-factor}}\]

Mole Concept

The term mole was suggested by Ostwald (Latin: mole = heap). A mole is the amount of substance containing the same number of elementary particles as the number of atoms in 12 g of C-12.

  • 1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number NA)
  • 1 mol atoms = gram atomic mass = 1 gram-atom
  • 1 mol molecules = gram molecular mass
  • At STP (T = 273 K, P = 1 atm): 1 mol gas = 22.4 L = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules
  • Loschmidt number: No. of molecules in 1 cm³ of ideal gas at STP = 2.69 × 10¹⁹
Mole Relations
\[\text{Number of moles} = \frac{\text{Amount in grams}}{\text{Molar mass}}\] \[\text{Number of molecules} = \text{moles} \times N_A\] \[\text{Volume at STP (in L)} = \text{moles} \times 22.4\]
  • 1 amu = \(\dfrac{1}{N_A}\) g = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1 Dalton
  • One mole of electrons weighs 0.55 mg (5.5 × 10⁻⁴ g)
  • NA molecules in 1 g compound = \(\dfrac{N_A}{\text{g-molar mass}}\)

Empirical & Molecular Formula, Stoichiometry

Empirical vs Molecular Formula
  • Empirical formula: Simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a molecule. e.g. CH for benzene (C₆H₆).
  • Molecular formula: Actual formula showing total atoms in a molecule. e.g. C₆H₆.
  • Relationship: \[\text{Molecular formula} = (\text{Empirical formula})^n, \quad n = \frac{\text{Molecular formula mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}\]
Stoichiometry

The relative proportions in which reactants react and products are formed. (Greek: "to measure an element").

  • Limiting reagent: Reactant completely consumed in the reaction — determines the extent of product formed.
  • Excess reagent: Reactant that remains unreacted after the reaction.
  • Per cent yield: \[\% \text{yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\]

Redox Reactions

Chemical reactions involving both oxidation and reduction simultaneously are called redox reactions ('red' from reduction, 'ox' from oxidation). Always accompanied by energy change (heat, light, or electricity).

Oxidation & Reduction

#OxidationReduction
(i) Addition of O or removal of H.
e.g. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; 2H₂S + O₂ → 2H₂O + 2S		 Addition of H or removal of O.
e.g. H₂S + Cl₂ → 2HCl + S; Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
(ii) Addition of electronegative element or removal of electropositive element. e.g. 2KI + Cl₂ → 2KCl + I₂ Addition of electropositive element or removal of electronegative element. e.g. SiCl₄ + 4Na → Si + 4NaCl
(iii) Loss of electrons (de-electronation). e.g. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ Gain of electrons (electronation). e.g. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
(iv) Increase in oxidation number Decrease in oxidation number
(v) Caused by an oxidising agent Caused by a reducing agent
Oxidants (Oxidising Agents)

Accept electrons; their oxidation number decreases in a redox reaction.

  • Molecules of most electronegative elements: O₂, O₃, halogens (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂).
  • Compounds with element in highest oxidation state: K₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄, HClO₄, H₂SO₄, KClO₃, Ce(SO₄)₂.
  • Oxides of metals and non-metals: MgO, CrO₃, CO₂, etc.
Reductants (Reducing Agents)

Give electrons; their oxidation number increases in a redox reaction.

  • All metals (Na, Al, Zn, etc.) and some non-metals (C, S, P, H₂).
  • Metallic hydrides: NaH, LiH, KH, CaH₂.
  • Compounds with element in lowest oxidation state: H₂C₂O₄, FeSO₄, Hg₂Cl₂, SnCl₂, H₂S, SO₂, Na₂S₂O₃.
  • SO₂, HNO₂, and H₂O₂ can act as both oxidant and reductant.
\[\text{Eq. wt. of oxidant/reductant} = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Change in oxidation number}}\]

For disproportionation reactions: Eq. wt. = sum of eq. wts. of two half-reactions.

e.g. 4H₃PO₃ → 3H₃PO₄ + PH₃  ⟹  Eq. wt. of H₃PO₃ = \(\dfrac{M}{2} + \dfrac{M}{6} = \dfrac{2M}{3}\)

Oxidation Number (Oxidation State)

The charge an atom appears to have when all other atoms are removed as ions. Can be positive, negative, zero, or fractional.

Rules for Determining Oxidation Number
  1. The algebraic sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound = 0; in an ion = charge on the ion.
  2. Elements in elementary state: oxidation number = 0 (e.g. He, Cl₂, S₈, P₄).
  3. F always = −1 in all compounds (most electronegative).
  4. O is usually −2; except in peroxides (−1, e.g. Na₂O₂), in OF₂ (+2), in O₂F₂ (+1).
  5. H is +1 in most compounds; −1 in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH₂, LiH, MgH₂).
  6. Alkali metals: +1; Alkaline earth metals: +2.
  7. Oxidation number of metal in amalgams = 0.
  8. In coordinate bonds (from less to more electronegative): +2 for less electronegative, −2 for more electronegative atom. If from more to less electronegative: contribution = 0 for both.
  9. For p-block elements (except F, O): highest ON = group number; lowest ON = group number − 8.
  10. Transition elements: lowest ON = number of ns electrons; highest ON = ns + (n−1)d unpaired electrons.

Types of Redox Reactions

TypeDescriptionExample
(i) Combination Two atoms/molecules combine to form a third 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
(ii) Decomposition Molecule breaks into two or more components 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g)
(iii a) Displacement (metal) A metal in compound displaced by another metal CuSO₄(aq) + Zn(s) → Cu(s) + ZnSO₄(aq)
(iii b) Displacement (non-metal) A non-metal displaced by metal/non-metal Mg(s) + 2H₂O(g) → Mg(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
(iv) Intermolecular Oxidation and reduction in two separate compounds SnCl₂ + 2FeCl₃ → SnCl₄ + 2FeCl₂
(v) Intramolecular Oxidation and reduction in a single compound 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂
(vi) Disproportionation (autoredox) Same element is both oxidised and reduced Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → ClO⁻ + Cl⁻ + H₂O; 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
Direct vs Indirect Redox Reactions
  • Direct Redox: Oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously in the same container; energy released as heat.
  • Indirect Redox: Oxidation and reduction occur separately in two half-cells; energy released as electrical energy (basis of electrochemical cells).

Balancing of Redox Equations

Two methods: (i) Ion Electron Method and (ii) Oxidation Number Method.

Method 1: Ion Electron Method (Half-Reaction Method)

Developed by Jette and Lamer (1927). Example: Cu + HNO₃(dil) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + NO + H₂O

  1. Write ionic form: Cu + H⁺ + NO₃⁻ → Cu²⁺ + NO + H₂O
  2. Split into oxidation and reduction half-reactions:
    • Oxidation: Cu → Cu²⁺
    • Reduction: NO₃⁻ → NO
  3. Balance atoms other than H and O.
  4. Balance O and H:
    • Acidic/neutral: Add H₂O to O-deficient side; add H⁺ to H-deficient side.
    • Alkaline: For each excess O, add H₂O to same side and OH⁻ to other side.
  5. Balance charge with electrons: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻  |  3e⁻ + 4H⁺ + NO₃⁻ → NO + 2H₂O
  6. Equalise electrons by multiplying by suitable numbers: ×3 and ×2.
  7. Add balanced half-reactions and cancel common terms.
  8. Convert to molecular form by adding spectator ions.

Final: 3Cu + 8HNO₃(dil) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O

Spectator ions: Ions present in solution but not taking part in the reaction are called spectator ions and are omitted in the net ionic equation.

Method 2: Oxidation Number Method

Example: Mg + HNO₃(dil) → Mg(NO₃)₂ + N₂O + H₂O

  1. Write skeleton equation.
  2. Assign oxidation numbers to all atoms and identify changes.
  3. Balance atoms other than H and O in oxidised and reduced species.
  4. Equalize total increase = total decrease in oxidation number by multiplying coefficients.
    • Mg: 0 → +2, change = 2; N: +5 → +1, change per N atom = 4; for N₂O, change = 8 (for 2 N atoms). So 4Mg : 1N₂O.
  5. Balance H and O to get: 4Mg + 10HNO₃ → 4Mg(NO₃)₂ + N₂O + 5H₂O

Redox Reactions in Daily Life

Oxidation Processes Involve:
  • Corrosion of metals
  • Bleaching (of clothes, paper)
  • Antiseptics (H₂O₂, Cl₂)
  • Combustion of fuel (wood, coal, petrol)
Reduction Processes Involve:
  • Photography (silver halide reduction)
  • Antioxidants (Vitamin C, E)
  • Photosynthesis (CO₂ → glucose)
  • Metallurgy (extraction of metals from ores)
