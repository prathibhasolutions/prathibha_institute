Electrochemistry

⚡ Introduction, Conductors & Electrolytes

Electrochemistry deals with production of electricity from spontaneous chemical reactions and the use of electrical energy to bring about non-spontaneous reactions.

Types of Conductors
  • Metallic/Electronic conductors: Conduct via movement of electrons. e.g. metals.
  • Electrolytic conductors: Conduct via ions in fused/aqueous state; undergo chemical decomposition. e.g. NaCl(aq), HCl(aq).
  • Superconductors: Zero resistivity/infinite conductivity. e.g. ceramic materials and mixed oxides at up to 150 K.
Strong vs Weak Electrolytes
  • Strong electrolytes: Completely dissociate into ions. e.g. KNO₃, KCl, NaOH, H₂SO₄.
  • Weak electrolytes: Partially ionise. e.g. CH₃COOH, NH₃, HCN.
Types of Cells
  • Electrochemical (Galvanic) cell: Chemical → electrical energy. Spontaneous redox reaction. e.g. Daniell cell.
  • Electrolytic cell: Electrical → chemical energy. Non-spontaneous reaction (electrolysis). Does not need salt bridge.
  • Standard cell (Weston cell): Cell of almost constant emf.
💡 Electrochemical Cell (Galvanic Cell)
Cell Representation & Daniell Cell
General representation: M₁(s) | M₁ⁿ⁺(aq) || M₂ⁿ⁺(aq) | M₂(s) Anode (−) LHS || Cathode (+) RHS Daniell Cell: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s) Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation) Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction) Net: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu; E°cell = 1.1 V

Salt Bridge & Transport Number
  • Salt bridge: U-shaped glass tube with agar-agar paste containing NH₄NO₃, KNO₃ or KCl (conducting electrolytes). KCl preferred because transport numbers of K⁺ and Cl⁻ are almost equal. KCl NOT used when electrode is Ag.
  • Functions: (1) Completes circuit; (2) Maintains electrical neutrality on both sides.
Transport number (transference number): nₓ = current carried by ion / total current Transport number of cation (nₓ) + Transport number of anion (nₐ) = 1
📈 Electrode Potential, EMF & Standard Hydrogen Electrode
Electrode Potential
  • Electrode potential: Tendency of electrode to lose or gain electrons when in contact with its ion solution. Expressed in volts. Intensive property.
  • Oxidation potential: Tendency to lose electrons (inversely proportional to concentration of ions).
  • Reduction potential: Tendency to gain electrons.
  • By IUPAC convention: Electrode potential = reduction potential unless stated.
E°red = −E°oxidation
Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE/NHE)
  • Pt wire + Pt foil coated with Pt black in 1 M HCl; H₂ gas bubbled at 1 atm, 298 K.
  • Half-cell: Pt, H₂(1 atm) | H⁺(1 M)
  • E°(SHE) = 0.00 V at all temperatures (defined standard).
Drawbacks:
  1. Difficult to maintain H₂ at exactly 1 atm.
  2. Difficult to maintain [H⁺] = 1 M.
  3. Pt electrode easily poisoned by impurities.
Alternative: Calomel electrode (Hg in contact with Hg₂Cl₂ paste in KCl solution). Used as secondary reference electrode.
EMF of a Cell
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = E°right − E°left = E°red + E°oxi EMF vs Cell Potential: EMF: potential difference when NO current flows; measured by potentiometer. Cell potential: potential difference when current flows (always < EMF); measured by voltmeter.

Applications of Electrochemical Series
  1. Lower E° → greater tendency to form cation (better reducing agent).
  2. Metals below H in ECS replace H₂ from dilute acids; metals above H cannot. (Cu + dil.H₂SO₄ → no reaction)
  3. Oxides of metals below H: NOT reduced by H₂. Oxides of metals above Fe: reduced by H₂.
  4. Reducing character increases down the series.
  5. emf = E°(RHS) − E°(LHS); positive emf = spontaneous reaction.
  6. Greater reduction potential → higher oxidising power (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂).
  7. Negative standard reduction potential → site of oxidation.
  8. Oxides of metals with E°red ≥ +0.79 V decompose on heating: e.g. HgO → Hg + ½O₂.
🧮 Nernst Equation & Concentration Cells
Nernst Equation
For electrode: Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ → M E = E° − (RT/nF) × log [1/[Mⁿ⁺]] E = E° − (0.0591/n) × log [1/[Mⁿ⁺]] at 298 K For cell: aA + bB → cC + dD Ecell = E°cell − (2.303RT/nF) × log ([C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ) Ecell = E°cell − (0.0591/n) × log Q at 298 K (Concentration of pure solids/liquids = 1)
Nernst Equation & Kc
At equilibrium: Ecell = 0 ∴ E°cell = (0.0591/n) × log Kc at 298 K ΔG° = −nFE°cell Relationship table: ΔG° | E°cell | Type −ve | +ve | Spontaneous (galvanic) +ve | −ve | Non-spontaneous (electrolytic) 0 | 0 | Equilibrium (dead battery) ΔG° = −2.303 RT log Kc
Concentration Cells

Both electrodes of same type; electrolytic solutions of different concentrations.

(i) Electrode concentration cells: Pt, H₂(p₁) | H⁺(1M) | H₂(p₂), Pt (p₁ > p₂) Ecell = (2.303RT/nF) × log(p₂/p₁) (ii) Electrolyte concentration cells: Zn | Zn²⁺(C₁) || Zn²⁺(C₂) | Zn (C₂ > C₁) Ecell = (0.0591/n) × log(C₂/C₁) at 298 K
📊 Conductance, Molar Conductivity & Kohlrausch's Law
Definitions
Conductance G = 1/R Units: ohm⁻¹ = Ω⁻¹ = mho = Siemens (S) Specific conductivity κ = (1/ρ) = G × (l/a) = G × cell constant Cell constant = l/a (units: cm⁻¹ or m⁻¹) Units of κ: S cm⁻¹ κ decreases on dilution (fewer ions per cc) Molar conductivity Λm = (κ × 1000) / M (M = molarity, units: Ω⁻¹ cm² mol⁻¹ or S cm² mol⁻¹) Equivalent conductivity Λeq = (κ × 1000) / N (N = normality)
Variation with Concentration
  • Strong electrolytes: Λm increases with dilution (interionic attractions decrease). Limiting value Λ°m obtained by extrapolating graph.
  • Weak electrolytes: Degree of ionisation increases on dilution → Λm increases sharply. Limiting value Λ°m cannot be obtained by graph extrapolation → use Kohlrausch's law.
Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation: Λm = Λ°m − A√C A = constant (depends on solvent nature, temperature, type of electrolyte)
Factors: Nature of electrolyte, concentration, temperature (increasing T → increases Λm).
Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions ★★★

At infinite dilution, molar conductivity of an electrolyte = sum of ionic conductivities of cations and anions.

Λ°m (AₓBᵧ) = x × λ°₊(A) + y × λ°₋(B) Applications: (i) Λ°m of weak electrolytes: Λ°(CH₃COOH) = Λ°(CH₃COONa) + Λ°(HCl) − Λ°(NaCl) Λ°(NH₄OH) = Λ°(NH₄Cl) + Λ°(NaOH) − Λ°(NaCl) (ii) Degree of dissociation (α): α = Λm(C) / Λ°m (iii) Dissociation constant: Kc = Cα² / (1−α) (iv) Solubility of sparingly soluble salts: Λ°m = κ × 1000 / solubility(mol/L) Solubility = κ × 1000 / Λ°m
⚡ Electrolysis & Faraday's Laws
Faraday's First Law

Amount of substance deposited at an electrode is directly proportional to quantity of electricity passed.

W ∝ Q = I × t W = Z × I × t = Z × Q Z = electrochemical equivalent = mass deposited by 1 A current for 1 sec (= 1 coulomb) 1 Faraday = 96500 C = charge on 1 mole of electrons
Faraday's Second Law

When same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, masses deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights.

W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂ (for same Q) Z₁/Z₂ = E₁/E₂ ∴ Electrochemical equivalent ∝ equivalent weight
Key Facts About Electrolysis
  • In electrolytic cell: both oxidation and reduction in same cell. Anode (+), Cathode (−).
  • In molten electrolyte: cations liberated at cathode, anions at anode.
  • When multiple ions compete: ion with higher reduction potential → liberated at cathode; ion with lower reduction potential → liberated at anode.
  • Overvoltage/bubble voltage: Extra voltage required beyond theoretical value for liberation of gases.
Predicting products of electrolysis (aqueous solution):
At cathode: if cation E° > −0.83 V (water) → cation deposited (e.g. Cu, Ag). Else H₂ liberated (for K, Na, Ca salts).
At anode: if anion E° > −1.23 V (water) → anion liberated (e.g. Br⁻). Else O₂ liberated (e.g. F⁻, SO₄²⁻ in Na₂SO₄ since E°(SO₄²⁻) = −0.2 V).
🔋 Batteries — Primary & Secondary
Primary Batteries (Non-rechargeable)
(i) Dry Cell (Leclanché cell):
Anode: Zinc container Cathode: Graphite rod (surrounded by MnO₂ powder) Electrolyte: Paste of NH₄Cl + ZnCl₂ Cathode: 2MnO₂ + 2NH₄⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mn₂O₃ + 2NH₃ + H₂O Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ Cell potential: 1.25–1.5 V
(ii) Mercury Cell:
Anode: Zn-Hg amalgam Cathode: Paste of HgO + C Electrolyte: Moist KOH-ZnO paste Cathode: HgO + H₂O + 2e⁻ → Hg(l) + 2OH⁻ Anode: Zn(Hg) + 2OH⁻ → ZnO(s) + H₂O + 2e⁻ Net: Zn(Hg) + HgO → ZnO + Hg Cell potential: 1.35 V (remains constant throughout life)
Constant potential because net reaction involves no ions whose concentration can change.
Secondary Batteries (Rechargeable)
(i) Lead Storage Battery:
Anode: Spongy lead Cathode: Grid of lead packed with PbO₂ Electrolyte: 38% H₂SO₄ (by mass) Anode: Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻ Cathode: PbO₂ + SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O Net: Pb + PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + 2SO₄²⁻ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O (Reactions reversed on recharging)
(ii) Nickel-Cadmium (Ni-Cd) cell:
Anode: Cadmium Cathode: Metal grid containing NiO₂ Electrolyte: KOH solution Anode: Cd + 2OH⁻ → Cd(OH)₂ + 2e⁻ Cathode: NiO₂ + 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → Ni(OH)₂ + 2OH⁻ Net: Cd + NiO₂ + 2H₂O → Cd(OH)₂ + Ni(OH)₂ Cell potential: 1.4 V
Ni-Cd cell has longer life than lead storage battery.
🔥 Fuel Cells

Galvanic cells using combustion energy of fuels (H₂, CH₄, CH₃OH, etc.) to produce electrical energy. Pollution-free, high efficiency (~70% vs ~40% for thermal plants).

Hydrogen-Oxygen Fuel Cell
Electrodes: Porous graphite impregnated with catalyst (Pt, Ag, or metal oxide) Electrolyte: Aqueous KOH or NaOH (H₂ and O₂ continuously fed) Anode: 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻ (oxidation) Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (reduction) Net: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l) EMF of the cell = 1 V Thermodynamic efficiency: η = (ΔG/ΔH) × 100% = −nFEcell/ΔH × 100%
🔴 Corrosion — Electrochemical Theory & Prevention

Corrosion: Slow formation of undesirable compounds (oxides, sulphides, carbonates) at metal surfaces by reaction with atmospheric moisture and gases.

Factors Affecting Corrosion
  1. Reactivity of metals
  2. Presence of moisture and atmospheric gases (CO₂, SO₂)
  3. Presence of impurities
  4. Strains in the metal
  5. Presence of electrolyte
Rusting of Iron — Electrochemical Theory
A corrosion cell forms at iron surface: Anode: Pure iron Cathode: Impure surface Electrolyte: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → 2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ Anode: 2Fe(s) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ Cathode: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l) Net: 2Fe + 4H⁺ + O₂ → 2Fe²⁺ + 2H₂O At surface: 4Fe²⁺ + O₂ + 8H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8H⁺ Fe₂O₃ + xH₂O → Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (hydrated iron oxide = RUST)
Prevention of Corrosion
  1. Barrier protection — coating with paints or electroplating (Ni, Cr).
  2. Galvanisation — coating with Zn (reactive metal oxidises preferentially protecting Fe).
  3. Tinning — coating with tin (but if tin layer breaks, Fe corrodes faster).
  4. Antirust solutions — bisphenol, etc.
  5. Cathodic protection (sacrificial anode) — connect to more easily oxidised metal (e.g. Mg or Zn block connected to buried iron pipelines). The Mg/Zn oxidises preferentially, protecting Fe.
