Chemical Kinetics

Rate of Reaction

Chemical Kinetics deals with the rate of chemical reactions, the factors affecting the rate, and the mechanism of reactions.

Types of Reactions Based on Rate
  • Fast/Instantaneous: Complete in less than 1 ps (10⁻¹² s). e.g. ionic reactions, organic substitution reactions.
  • Slow: Complete in minutes to years. e.g. rusting of iron, transformation of diamond to graphite.
  • Moderately slow: Intermediate between fast and slow.
Average Rate of Reaction

Rate of reaction = change in concentration of reactant/product per unit time. Units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ or Ms⁻¹.

For the reaction \(aA + bB \rightarrow cC + dD\):

\[r_{av} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{\Delta[A]}{\Delta t} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{\Delta[B]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\Delta[C]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{1}{d}\frac{\Delta[D]}{\Delta t}\]

The slowest step of a reaction is called the rate determining step (van't Hoff).

Instantaneous Rate of Reaction

Rate at a particular moment in time. For \(R \rightarrow P\):

\[r_{inst} = -\frac{d[R]}{dt} = +\frac{d[P]}{dt}\]

Methods to measure rate: pH measurement, change in optical activity, change in pressure, change in conductance.

Factors affecting rate: Nature and concentration of reactants, temperature, surface area, radiation, catalyst, pressure.

Rate Law & Rate Constant

For \(aA + bB \rightarrow \text{Products}\), the experimentally determined rate law is:

\[\text{Rate} = k[A]^\alpha[B]^\beta\]

where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are experimental orders (not necessarily equal to stoichiometric coefficients), and \(k\) is the rate constant (velocity constant).

Rate Constant \(k\)

Rate \(= k\) when all concentrations = 1 mol L⁻¹. Units of \(k\) for an \(n\)th order reaction:

\[k = \frac{1}{\text{time}} \times \frac{1}{[\text{Conc.}]^{n-1}}\]
OrderUnit of \(k\)
Zero (n = 0)mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
First (n = 1)s⁻¹
Second (n = 2)L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
Third (n = 3)L² mol⁻² s⁻¹

Characteristics of \(k\): Greater \(k\) → faster reaction. \(k\) is specific to each reaction, changes with temperature but NOT with concentration.

Types of Reactions

By Order:

  • Zero order: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (hν); 2NH₃ → N₂ + 3H₂ (Pt)
  • First order: H₂O₂ → H₂O + ½O₂; Radioactive decay; Inversion of cane sugar
  • Second order: 2HI → H₂ + I₂; Alkaline hydrolysis of esters (saponification)
  • Third order: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂

By Molecularity:

  • Unimolecular: N₂O₄(g) → 2NO₂(g)
  • Bimolecular: 2HI(g) → H₂(g) + I₂(g)
  • Trimolecular: 2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g)

Integrated Rate Equations

Zero Order Reactions

Rate is independent of concentration:

\[k_0 = \frac{[A]_0 - [A]}{t}\]
  • Half-life: \(t_{1/2} = \dfrac{[A]_0}{2k_0}\)   (proportional to initial concentration)
  • Unit of \(k_0\): mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (same as rate)
  • For gaseous reactions: \(k_0 = \dfrac{p_0 - p}{t}\),   \(t_{1/2} = \dfrac{p_0}{2k_0}\)
First Order Reactions

Rate proportional to first power of concentration:

\[k_1 = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{[A]_0}{[A]} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad [A] = [A]_0 e^{-k_1 t}\]
  • Half-life: \(t_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{k_1}\)   (concentration-independent)
  • Amount after \(n\) half-lives: \(\dfrac{[A]_0}{2^n}\)
  • \(t_{75\%} = 2 \times t_{50\%}\)  |  \(t_{99.9\%} = 10 \times t_{1/2}\)
  • All radioactive changes follow first order kinetics.
  • For gaseous first order: \(A(g) \rightarrow B(g) + C(g)\): \[k_1 = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{p_0}{2p_0 - p_t}\]
  • For ester hydrolysis (using NaOH titration volumes): \[k_1 = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{V_\infty - V_0}{V_\infty - V_t}\]
Pseudo First Order Reaction

Reactions that appear higher order but behave as first order because one reactant is in large excess.

e.g. Ester hydrolysis: CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH

Rate = \(k\)[CH₃COOC₂H₅] (water is in excess, so its concentration is effectively constant).

For Inversion of cane sugar (using polarimetric readings \(r_0, r_t, r_\infty\)): \[k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{r_0 - r_\infty}{r_t - r_\infty}\]

Methods to Determine Order of Reaction

MethodDescription
(i) Graphical Method Plot rate vs concentration: straight line through origin = zero order; rate vs [A] = first order; rate vs [A]² = second order.
(ii) Initial Rate Method Vary concentration of one reactant while keeping others constant. Determine order from ratio of rates.
(iii) Integrated Rate Law Method Use the integrated rate equation that gives the most constant value of \(k\).
(iv) Half-life Method \(t_{1/2} \propto \dfrac{1}{[A]_0^{n-1}}\). Plot \(t_{1/2}\) vs \([A]_0\) (zero order: linear), vs \([A]_0\) (first order: constant), vs \(1/[A]_0\) (second order: linear).
(v) Ostwald's Isolation Method Isolate one reactant at a time (keep all others in large excess) to determine its partial order.

Temperature Dependence & Arrhenius Equation

For every 10°C rise in temperature, the rate of reaction approximately doubles (only 16% more collisions, but exponentially more effective ones).

Temperature coefficient: \(\dfrac{k_{t+10}}{k_t} \approx 2 \text{ to } 3\) (usually at 298 K and 308 K).

Arrhenius Equation
\[k = Ae^{-E_a/RT}\]

Taking logarithm:

\[\log_{10} k = \log_{10} A - \frac{E_a}{2.303RT}\]

A plot of \(\log_{10} k\) vs \(1/T\) gives a straight line with:

  • Slope \(= -\dfrac{E_a}{2.303R}\)
  • Intercept \(= \log_{10} A\)
SymbolMeaning
\(A\)Frequency factor (pre-exponential factor, Arrhenius factor)
\(E_a\)Activation energy (J mol⁻¹)
\(R\)Gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
Key Results from Arrhenius Equation
  • Rate constant at two temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\): \[\log\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{2.303R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right)\]
  • Boltzmann factor (fraction of molecules with energy ≥ \(E_a\)): \[x = \frac{n}{N} = e^{-E_a/RT} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \log_{10} x = \frac{-E_a}{2.303RT}\]
  • \(E_a\) is constant for a given reaction — independent of T, V, P — but affected by catalyst.
  • When \(T \to \infty\) or \(E_a = 0\): \(k = A\) (rate independent of temperature).
  • Lower the activation energy \(E_a\), faster the reaction.
Activated Complex (Transition State)

The highest-energy, unstable intermediate between reactants and products — bonds of reactants are not completely broken and bonds of products are not completely formed.

\[E_a = E_T - E_R\]
  • \(E_T\) = Threshold energy (minimum energy reactants must possess to form products)
  • \(E_R\) = Energy of reactants
  • Larger \(E_a\) → smaller \(k\) → greater effect of temperature rise on rate.
  • A catalyst lowers \(E_a\), providing an alternate reaction pathway: \[\frac{k_P}{k_a} = e^{(E_a - E_P)/RT} = e^{\Delta E/RT}\]

Collision Theory & Photochemical Reactions

Collision Theory of Reaction Rates

Reactant molecules are treated as hard spheres. Reaction occurs only when molecules collide with sufficient energy and proper orientation.

  • Collision frequency (\(Z_{AB}\)): Number of collisions per second per unit volume.
  • Effective/fruitful collisions: Only those with energy ≥ \(E_a\) and correct orientation.
\[\text{Rate} = PZ_{AB} e^{-E_a/RT}\]

where \(P\) = steric factor (probability/orientation factor), \(Z_{AB}\) = collision frequency, \(e^{-E_a/RT}\) = fraction of molecules with energy ≥ \(E_a\).

Activated Complex (Transition State) Theory

Reactants ⇌ Activated complex → Products

Bond cleavage and formation occur simultaneously. Reactants are converted to an intermediate activated complex (with partially formed bonds) before forming products. The height of the energy barrier = threshold energy.

Photochemical Reactions

Reactions that occur on exposure to visible radiation.

  • Rate is affected by intensity of light.
  • Temperature has little effect (unlike thermal reactions).
  • Quantum yield (φ): \[\phi = \frac{\text{Number of reactant molecules reacting in a given time}}{\text{Number of photons (quanta) absorbed in the same time}}\]
