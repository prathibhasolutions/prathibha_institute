Chemistry Handbook — Chapter 21

Coordination Compounds

Complete Notes · Arihant Handbook of Chemistry

🧬 Introduction & Basic Terminology

Coordination compounds are molecular compounds that retain their identity in solid state as well as in dissolved state. The central metal atom/ion is linked by ions or molecules with coordinate bonds.

Double salts vs Coordination compounds: Double salts (e.g. Mohr's salt) are stable in solid state but dissociate into constituent ions in solution. Coordination compounds retain the complex entity in solution.

Key Terms
  • Complex Ion / Coordination Entity: Electrically charged species where central metal atom/ion is surrounded by ions or neutral molecules (ligands). e.g. [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, [Pt(NH₃)₄]²⁺
  • Central Atom/Ion: Atom or ion to which ligands are bound; acts as a Lewis acid. Usually a transition/inner-transition element.
  • Coordination Sphere: Central metal/ion + attached ligands enclosed in square brackets [ ]. Ionisable groups written outside = counter ions.
  • Coordination Polyhedron: Spatial arrangement of ligands attached to the central atom. e.g. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ is octahedral; [Ni(CO)₄] is tetrahedral; [PtCl₄]²⁻ is square planar.
  • Oxidation Number: Charge on complex if all ligands are removed with their electron pairs. e.g. in [Cu(CN)₄]³⁻, Cu = +1.
  • Homoleptic: Metal linked to only one kind of donor atoms. e.g. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺
  • Heteroleptic: Metal linked to more than one kind of donor atoms. e.g. [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺

Coordination Number
  • = Number of coordinate bonds formed by central metal with ligands.
  • For monodentate ligands: CN = number of ligands.
  • For polydentate: CN = number of ligands × denticity.
  • e.g. [PtCl₆]²⁻: CN = 6 (Pt); [Ni(en)₃]²⁺: CN = 6 (Ni, en is bidentate)
Effective Atomic Number (EAN)

Proposed by Sidgwick. EAN = total number of electrons in a complex.

EAN = Z − ON + 2 × CN Z = atomic number of metal ON = oxidation number of metal CN = coordination number Stable complex: EAN = atomic number of next noble gas. e.g. [Fe(CO)₅]: Z(Fe)=26, ON=0, CN=5 EAN = 26 − 0 + 2×5 = 36 (Kr) → stable!
Labile vs Inert Complexes
  • Labile complexes: Ligand substitution is fast (half-life < 30 s at 25°C). e.g. most Co(II), Ni(II), Cu(II) complexes.
  • Inert complexes: Ligand substitution is slow. e.g. Cr(III) and Co(III) complexes. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ is kinetically inert.
📝 IUPAC Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds
Rules
  1. Name: Cation first, then anion (in both positively and negatively charged complexes).
  2. Within complex: Ligands in alphabetical order before name of metal ion.
  3. Multiple identical ligands: di, tri, tetra; if name contains di/tri etc., use bis, tris, tetrakis.
  4. Anionic complex: metal name ends in –ate (with Latin name).
  5. Anionic ligands: end in –o; Positive ligands: end in –ium; Neutral: unchanged.
  6. Exceptions: H₂O = aqua; NH₃ = ammine; CO = carbonyl; NO = nitrosyl.
  7. Oxidation state: Roman numeral in parentheses after metal name.

🔀 Isomerism in Coordination Compounds
1. Structural Isomerism

Isomers with different bonding patterns.

(i) Linkage isomerism — ambidentate ligands:
[Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]Cl₂ nitrito-N (via N) [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]Cl₂ nitrito-O (via O) Both have same molecular formula, different donor atom.
(ii) Coordination isomerism — interchange of ligands between cationic and anionic coordination entities:
[Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆] [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆]
(iii) Ionisation isomerism — exchange of ionisable anion with anionic ligand:
[Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br (Red) [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ (Violet)
(iv) Solvate/Hydrate isomerism — different number of solvent molecules inside/outside coordination sphere:
[Co(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ [Co(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl·2H₂O [Co(H₂O)₃Cl₃]·3H₂O
2. Stereoisomerism

Same chemical formula and bonds, different spatial arrangement.

(i) Geometrical isomerism (cis–trans):
  • Tetrahedral complexes: NO geometrical isomers.
  • Square planar [MX₂L₂]: cis (X adjacent) and trans (X opposite). e.g. [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]
  • Square planar [MABXL]: 3 isomers (2 cis + 1 trans)
  • Octahedral [MX₂L₄]: cis and trans. e.g. [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺
  • Octahedral [MA₃X₃]: fac (facial) — 3 same ligands on triangular face; mer (meridional) — 3 same ligands on meridian. e.g. [Co(NH₃)₃(NO₂)₃]
(ii) Optical isomerism:
  • Chiral structures whose mirror images cannot be superimposed.
  • Mirror images = enantiomers; d (dextro) and l (laevo) forms.
  • Tetrahedral [M(AB)₂]: show optical isomers.
  • Octahedral cis complexes: exhibit optical isomerism.
⚛️ Bonding in Coordination Compounds
Werner's Theory
  • Primary valency = oxidation number (ionisable, non-directional, satisfied by anions).
  • Secondary valency = coordination number (non-ionisable, directional, satisfied by ligands → decides geometry).
  • Metal ion must satisfy BOTH primary and secondary valencies.
Limitation: Could not explain geometry, magnetic properties, optical properties.
Valence Bond Theory (VBT) — L. Pauling, 1930s

Metal ion provides empty hybridised orbitals to receive electron pairs from ligands.

Inner Orbital Complexes (low spin):
  • Formed by strong field/low spin ligands.
  • Use (n–1)d orbitals → hybridisation = d²sp³ (octahedral).
  • e.g. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺: d²sp³, diamagnetic (all paired).
Outer Orbital Complexes (high spin):
  • Formed by weak field/high spin ligands.
  • Use nd orbitals → hybridisation = sp³d² (octahedral).
  • e.g. [CoF₆]³⁻: sp³d², paramagnetic (unpaired electrons).

Limitations of VBT: Cannot explain quantised magnetic data, colour of complexes, temperature variation of magnetic moment, relative strength of ligands.
Crystal Field Theory (CFT) — H. Bethe (1929), van Vleck & Orgel (1935)

Ligands are treated as point charges (anions) or dipoles (neutral molecules). Their electrostatic field causes splitting of degenerate d-orbitals.

The Five d-orbitals Split into Two Sets:
t₂g orbitals: dxy, dyz, dzx (oriented BETWEEN coordinate axes) eg orbitals: dx²-y², dz² (oriented ALONG coordinate axes)
In Octahedral Field:
6 ligands approach along x, y, z axes → eg orbitals repelled MORE (face ligands directly) → Energy of eg raises by +3/5 Δₒ → Energy of t₂g lowers by −2/5 Δₒ → Crystal field splitting = Δₒ (or 10Dq)
High Spin vs Low Spin:
If Δₒ < P (pairing energy): → 4th electron enters eg → t₂g³ eg¹ (high spin, weak field) If Δₒ > P (pairing energy): → 4th electron enters t₂g → t₂g⁴ eg⁰ (low spin, strong field)
In Tetrahedral Field:
Splitting INVERTED: t₂ > e Δₜ = (4/9) Δₒ (smaller than octahedral) → Always HIGH SPIN (Δₜ always < P)
Spectrochemical Series (Increasing Crystal Field Strength)
I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < EDTA⁴⁻ < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO Weak field ←————————————————————————→ Strong field (high spin) (low spin)
CO and CN⁻ are strong field (π-acid) ligands; F⁻ and I⁻ are weak field ligands. Ligands beyond NH₃ cause low spin in octahedral complexes.
Colour in CFT: d-d transition of electrons from t₂g → eg level. Absorbed light wavelength determines colour. [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ = violet; [Cu(H₂O)₄]²⁺ = blue; [Ni(H₂O)₆]²⁺ = green.
In absence of ligands, no crystal field splitting → colourless. Charge transfer also gives intense colour (e.g. MnO₄⁻: purple from O→Mn transfer).
Limitations of CFT:
  • Does not consider formation of π bonding in complexes.
  • Cannot explain why H₂O is stronger ligand than OH⁻.
  • No account of partial covalent character of metal–ligand bonds.
Ligand Field / Molecular Orbital Theory (Hund & Mulliken)
All atomic orbitals of participating atoms mix to give an equivalent number of new orbitals (molecular orbitals). Electrons are under the influence of all nuclei. This theory overcomes limitations of both VBT and CFT.
⚖️ Stability of Coordination Compounds
M^x+ + nL^y⁻ ⇌ [ML_n]^(x-ny) Stability constant Kf = [ [MLₙ]^(x-ny) ] / [M^x+][L^y-]^n Instability (dissociation) constant Kd = 1/Kf

Factors affecting stability:

  • Charge on central metal atom: Higher charge → more stable complex.
  • Nature of metal ion: Stability order: 3d < 4d < 5d series.
  • Basic nature of ligands: Strong field/better donor ligands → more stable. More chelate rings → more stable (chelate effect).
💡 Importance & Applications of Coordination Compounds
  • Used in qualitative and quantitative analysis (EDTA titration for water hardness).
  • Na₂EDTA used for estimation of hardness of water.
  • Purification of metals via formation and decomposition of complexes (e.g. Ni via [Ni(CO)₄] — Mond process).
  • Great importance in biological systems.
  • Used as catalysts: Wilkinson's catalyst [Rh(PPh₃)₃Cl] — homogeneous hydrogenation; Ziegler-Natta catalyst [TiCl₄ + (C₂H₅)₃Al] — ethylene polymerisation.
  • Chelating therapy in medicine.
Biologically Important Complexes:
  • Haemoglobin: Fe²⁺ in porphyrin ring (octahedral; O₂ as ligand in lungs)
  • Chlorophyll: Mg²⁺ in porphyrin ring (photosynthesis)
  • Vitamin B₁₂ (cyanocobalamin): Co³⁺ complex
  • Cisplatin [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]: cancer treatment (cis isomer active)
🧪 Organometallic Compounds

Compounds containing one or more metal–carbon (M–C) bonds. Classified into:

σ-bonded Compounds
M–C bond is sigma bond.
e.g. (C₂H₅)₄Pb, Zn(C₂H₅)₂, R–Mg–X (Grignard reagent)
π-bonded Compounds
π-bonds act as ligands.
Zeise's salt: K[PtCl₃(η²-C₂H₄)] — ethylene as ligand without lone pair.
Ferrocene: Fe(η⁵-C₅H₅)₂ — η means 5 carbons directly bonded to Fe.
Dibenzene chromium: Cr(η⁶-C₆H₆)₂
σ + π bonded (Metal Carbonyls)
M–C bond has both σ and π character. CO = ligand.
Ni(CO)₄ — tetrahedral, Ni is 0 Fe(CO)₅ — trigonal bipyramidal, Fe is 0 Cr(CO)₆ — octahedral, Cr is 0
